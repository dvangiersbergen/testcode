# Domain 3: Crime & Public Safety

**Research date:** 2026-02-22
**Evidence covers:** Crime rates (controlled and uncontrolled), victimisation, sexual assault, terrorism, perception gap, national statistics (Germany PKS, Sweden BRÅ, Netherlands CBS)
**Primary sources:** Peer-reviewed journals (JEP, AER, RES, AEJ:Applied, JEEA), BKA/PKS, BRÅ, CBS Netherlands, ICCT

**⚠ Domain note:** This is the most politically charged domain in the research project. Raw statistics without demographic controls are routinely weaponised in public debate. This report presents controlled and uncontrolled findings separately and explicitly, always noting what controls are and are not applied. Neither side of the political debate is served by omission.

---

## Evidence Summary

The best available causal evidence — from quasi-experimental and natural-experiment studies across Italy, Germany, Sweden, and the UK — finds that immigration does **not significantly increase local violent crime rates** when demographic controls are applied. Raw police and court statistics across all EU countries show overrepresentation of non-native suspects, but this overrepresentation is substantially explained by demographic confounders (younger age, higher male share, lower income, urban concentration). The most robust single predictor of immigrant crime propensity is **legal status**: legalization reduces recidivism by approximately 50% in Italian natural experiment studies, because it raises the opportunity cost of crime by enabling formal employment. Some evidence exists of modest increases in property crime and drug offences associated with specific subgroups (asylum seekers near large reception centres; recognised refugees in Germany). Migrants are simultaneously high-risk *victims* of crime, hate crime, and sexual violence — a dimension systematically absent from public debate. EU terrorism is predominantly a homegrown phenomenon (81% of French jihadist terrorism convictions: French nationals); irregular migration has contributed a minority of perpetrators.

## Key Findings

### 3.1 Controlled Academic Studies — The Causal Evidence Base

**Finding (landmark international review):** Despite immigrant overrepresentation in offender statistics — driven by being younger, male, and less educated — **immigration does not significantly impact local crime rates** across methodological approaches. Global homicide rates fell by one-third between 1990–2019 even as the immigrant population share grew by two-thirds. Legal status is the critical moderating variable.
- **Source:** Marie & Pinotti — *"Immigration and Crime: An International Perspective"*, *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, Vol. 38(1), Winter 2024, pp. 181–200. Covers 55 countries; panel of 216 European regions across 23 countries, 2002–2017; shift-share IV; fully controlled.
- **URL:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.38.1.181
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — top-tier peer-reviewed journal; 216 European regions; rigorous identification

**Finding (UK natural experiment):** Asylum seekers linked to a **modest rise in property crime** (consistent with restricted labour market access); EU accession migrants (A8, post-2004) linked to a **small negative effect** on crime. **No significant effect on violent crime** for either group. Controlling for personal and locality characteristics, foreign nationals are *slightly less likely* to report being a crime victim than British nationals.
- **Source:** Bell, Fasani & Machin — *"Crime and Immigration: Evidence from Large Immigrant Waves"*, *Review of Economics and Statistics*, Vol. 95(4), pp. 1278–1290 (2013). Two quasi-natural experiments; difference-in-differences; fully controlled.
- **URL:** https://direct.mit.edu/rest/article/95/4/1278/58317/Crime-and-Immigration-Evidence-from-Large
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — natural experiment; peer-reviewed; two independent identification strategies

**Finding (Germany, quasi-random dispersal):** Government-mandated dispersal of ethnic German repatriates (*Aussiedler*) provided exogenous variation. Immigration increased crime in regions with high unemployment, high population density, high pre-existing crime, and large foreign-born shares. Mechanism: poor language skills, low labour market attachment, and reduced deportation risk (these migrants held German citizenship). **Important caveat:** Specific to ethnic German repatriates — legally and socially unique — and **should not be generalised** to asylum seekers or labour migrants.
- **Source:** Piopiunik & Ruhose — *European Economic Review*, Vol. 92, pp. 258–282 (2017). Fully controlled.
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0014292116302252
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — exogenous dispersal; controlled; 🟧 limited generalisability

**Finding — legal status effect (Italy, mass clemency + EU enlargement):** Legal status reduces recidivism by approximately **50%**. Effect concentrated in areas with better labour market opportunities for legal immigrants. Mechanism: legal status raises the opportunity cost of crime by enabling formal employment. Tighter immigration quotas push immigrants toward illegality and thereby raise crime propensity.
- **Source:** Mastrobuoni & Pinotti — *"Legal Status and the Criminal Activity of Immigrants"*, *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 7(2), pp. 175–206 (2015). Individual-level prison data; difference-in-differences; fully controlled.
- **URL:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2438682
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — natural experiment; individual-level data; peer-reviewed; replicated by Fasani (2018) and Pinotti (2017) using independent Italian datasets

**Finding — repeated amnesty programs (Italy):** Regions where a higher share of immigrants obtained legal status experienced a greater decline in non-EU immigrant crime rates in the year following each amnesty. Effect is statistically significant but modest and not persistent over time.
- **Source:** Fasani — *"Immigrant Crime and Legal Status: Evidence from Repeated Amnesty Programs"*, *Journal of Economic Geography*, Vol. 18(4), pp. 887–914 (2018)
- **URL:** https://academic.oup.com/joeg/article-abstract/18/4/887/5039697
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — instrumental variable; seven legalisation programmes; peer-reviewed

### 3.2 Germany Post-2015 — Controlled Studies

**Finding (Germany, 401 counties, post-2014 refugee influx):** A one standard deviation increase in asylum seeker inflows is associated with approximately **123 additional crimes per 100,000** (~1.9% increase). Increase driven primarily by **drug offences and non-violent property crime** — not violent crime. No displacement of native workers. Counterintuitively, areas with larger refugee inflows showed *smaller* electoral gains for the far-right AfD.
- **Source:** Gehrsitz & Ungerer — *Economica*, Vol. 89(355), pp. 592–626 (2022). Quasi-random geographic assignment of asylum seekers to counties; county fixed effects; controlled.
- **URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/ecca.12420
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — quasi-random assignment; panel data; peer-reviewed

**Finding (Germany, distinguishing asylum seekers vs. recognised refugees):** Asylum seekers: **no causal impact on crime** except migration-specific offences. Recognised refugees: **positive association with overall crime**, driven by non-violent property crimes and fraud. No indirect crime response (backlash crimes) by German citizens. Mechanism: lower educational attainment and language skills increase property crime propensity when formal work access is limited.
- **Source:** Dehos — *Regional Science and Urban Economics*, Vol. 88(C) (2021). Exploits dispersal policies and location restrictions as natural experiment; distinguishes two migrant subgroups; controlled.
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0166046220303252
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — distinct identification strategies for each group; peer-reviewed

**Finding (Germany, district-level, 2018–2023):** "No correlation between an increasing share of foreigners in a district and the local crime rate." Apparent overrepresentation attributable to demographic confounders (youth, male-skewed population, urban concentration, poverty).
- **Source:** ZEW Discussion Paper 23-047; ifo Institute analysis. District-level regression; controlled for age, sex, poverty, urbanisation.
- **URL:** https://www.zew.de/fileadmin/FTP/dp/dp23047.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — working paper (not yet peer-published at time of research); consistent with peer-reviewed literature

### 3.3 Raw National Statistics — Uncontrolled (Read with Caution)

The following are official statistics presented **without demographic controls**. They document facts about the criminal justice system but cannot be treated as evidence of immigration causing crime.

#### Germany — PKS (Polizeiliche Kriminalstatistik)

**PKS 2023 (uncontrolled raw figures):**
- 41% of all crime suspects were non-German nationals, while foreigners are ~15% of the population
- Non-German suspects rose **13.5%** from 2022 vs. 1% for German suspects (excluding immigration-law violations)
- Violent crime suspects: +14.5% non-German vs. +2.2% German

**Mandatory methodological warnings for PKS data:**
1. PKS classifies by citizenship, not migration background. Naturalised immigrants and their German-born children are counted as "German" — this undercounts migrants in the "German" category and understates how much of the migrant/native gap is a generational integration gap.
2. Immigration-law violations (e.g., illegal entry) can *only* be committed by non-Germans. When excluded, migrant suspect numbers drop by more than half.
3. Non-German nationals skew younger, more male, poorer, and more urban — all independent crime risk factors. The ifo Institute finds no correlation between foreign-national share and crime rates after controlling for these demographics.
4. A widely circulated claim that "58.5% of violent crimes in Germany are committed by men with migrant background" is **factually false** — it was a misquotation of a statistic that actually referred to German citizens. (Debunked: https://eufactcheck.eu/factcheck/false-almost-60-of-violent-crimes-in-germany-are-committed-by-men-with-migrant-background/)
- **Source:** BKA PKS 2022/2023 — Tier 1 raw data
- **Evidence strength for causal claims:** 🟥 Insufficient — uncontrolled raw data

#### Sweden — BRÅ (Brottsförebyggande rådet)

**BRÅ 2005 (partial controls):**
- Foreign-born persons were **2.5 times more likely** to be registered as crime suspects than native Swedes (1997–2001 data)
- When socioeconomic factors (income, education, gender, age) are held constant, the gap narrows significantly
- First-generation immigrants who arrived in childhood have lower crime rates than adult arrivals
- Those born in North Africa and the Middle East had the highest overrepresentation; those from Western Europe and Southeast Asia the lowest

**Methodological warning:** The 2005 BRÅ used adult income and education as controls — criticised because these are themselves affected by prior criminal records, potentially understating the socioeconomic effect. The Swedish government declined a 2017 request to update this report. No comprehensive updated BRÅ report with demographic controls has been published for 2021–2023.

**Trend data (controlled, 1973–2017):** Overrepresentation of foreign-born among convicted persons has been **declining** over the period 1973–2017 (Hällsten et al., 2021). Second-generation immigrants show lower overrepresentation than first-generation.
- **Source:** Hällsten et al. (2021), *Scandinavian Journal of Criminal Law and Criminology* — longitudinal registry data
- **URL:** https://www.scup.com/doi/10.1080/2578983X.2021.1900038
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the trend (longitudinal registry data)

**Municipality-level study (2024, controlled):** The proportion of immigrants in a Swedish municipality had **only a small association** with the rate of reported violent crime. Nearly all municipalities had higher violent crime in 2020 vs. 2000 regardless of immigration levels.
- **Source:** *Journal of International Migration and Integration*, Springer (2024)
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12134-024-01221-1
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — retrospective case-control; controlled comparison

#### Netherlands — CBS

**CBS 2018 (uncontrolled):** Male migrants suspected of crime at roughly **2.5 times the rate** of native Dutch males. Rates vary widely by origin (East Asians: low; Netherlands Antilleans: 3.81 relative rate). **Trend:** Crime suspect rates for all groups have fallen approximately **50% since 2005**, for both native Dutch and non-western origin groups alike.

**Controlled self-report study (Netherlands):** When age, sex, urbanisation, and SES are controlled, both minority groups showed **similar to lower self-reported crime rates** than the majority group — an "ethnic paradox." First-generation immigrants in particular reported fewer crimes than expected given their social disadvantage (a "righteous migrant effect").
- **Source:** Leerkes et al., multiple studies including SAGE (2025)
- **URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14773708241307776
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — self-report crime surveys; social desirability bias acknowledged

### 3.4 Victimisation — Migrants as Crime Victims

This dimension is systematically under-discussed in public debate. Key findings:

| Country/context | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| UK | Foreign nationals slightly *less* likely to report victimisation than British nationals (controlling for locality) | Bell et al. (2013) |
| Netherlands | Non-western background persons more likely to be crime victims than native Dutch (24% vs. 19%), gap persistent over time | CBS (2018) |
| Germany 2023 | 2,488 attacks on refugees; 15,087 xenophobic crimes — a 50% increase from 2022 | PKS 2023 |
| Germany 2022 | 1,420 politically motivated attacks on asylum seekers/refugees; 83% far-right motivated | German government data |
| EU-wide | Migrants experience sexual violence before, during, and after migration; see section 3.5 | PMC synthesis (2018) |

### 3.5 Sexual Assault: Both Perpetration and Victimisation

**Finding — migrants as victims (systematic review):** Sexual violence is **highly prevalent** among migrants, asylum applicants, and refugees (MARs) in Europe, occurring before, during, and after migration. Among clinical patient samples: 27.6% reported sexual assault (37.6% of women, 7.3% of men); rape reported by 14.9% of clinical patients. Crucially: **21.1% of reported rapes and 17.7% of sexual assaults took place after arrival in the host country**, indicating continued victimisation risk in EU receiving countries.
- **Source:** PMC/NIH systematic synthesis — 25 peer-reviewed studies + 22 grey-literature documents across Europe (2018)
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6165364/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the prevalence finding; 🟫 Moderate on the within-EU-arrival proportion (clinical sample bias acknowledged)

**Cologne New Year's Eve 2015–16 (documented incident, not a controlled study):**
- 1,182 criminal reports; 497 sexual violence complaints; 648 victims
- 183 identified suspects: 55 Moroccan, 53 Algerian, 22 Iraqi, 14 Syrian, 14 German
- 73 suspects were asylum seekers; ~50% of identified suspects arrived in Germany in 2015
- **Status:** This is a documented criminal incident, not a statistically representative sample. It is appropriately cited as evidence of a real event; it is not appropriate to use it as evidence of a systematic national pattern without controlled data.
- **Academic analysis:** The Cologne events constituted a documented "discursive shift" in immigration media coverage, tapping into Orientalist stereotyping and contributing to racialisation of immigration discourse (SAGE, 2022)
- **Source:** German police and judicial records; VU Research Portal academic analysis
- **Evidence strength for causal inference about immigration and sexual assault broadly:** 🟥 Insufficient — single incident; not controlled

**General methodological challenge:** EU-wide statistics on sexual assault by perpetrator immigration status are not compiled by Eurostat. Country-level data varies in quality and what it records. Most EU studies on sexual assault do not systematically record perpetrator immigration status. This is a genuine data gap, not a political choice to hide findings.

### 3.6 Terrorism — Distinct from Ordinary Crime

**Finding (France, 2017–2021):** **81% of individuals incarcerated for jihadist terrorism-related offences** were French nationals; 86% were European nationals overall. EU terrorism is predominantly a **homegrown** phenomenon.
- **Source:** ICCT — *"Migration-related Terrorism: Trends, Challenges, and Policy Implications"* (2025)
- **URL:** https://icct.nl/sites/default/files/2025-07/Migration%20and%20Terrorism%20short%20June.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the French data; 🟫 Moderate on EU-wide generalisation (data quality varies)

**Finding (EU-wide):** A large majority of perpetrators of terrorist attacks in Europe over the last decade were **EU citizens**, not recent migrants or refugees.
- **Source:** Falkentoft & Crone, DIIS (Danish Institute for International Studies)
- **URL:** https://www.diis.dk/en/research/european-citizens-not-refugees-behind-most-terrorist-attacks-in-europe
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — institutional research; consistent with ICCT finding

**Finding (irregular migration and terrorism, 2014–2020):** Irregular immigrants were involved in 16% of terrorist incidents in Europe 2014–2020, rising to 25% in 2020 (France: from 0% pre-2017 to 33% by 2020). At least 104 Islamist extremists entered the EU via irregular migration 2014–2018; 28 completed attacks killing 170 people.
- **Sources:** Start Insight Report; Center for Immigration Studies (Tier 3 — restrictionist orientation)
- **Orientation note:** CIS is restrictionist; these figures should be cross-checked against independent sources
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak — case-counting methodology; not a controlled study; Tier 3 source with disclosed orientation

**Large-N academic studies on immigration → terrorism:** Bove & Böhmelt (2016, 145 countries): increases in migrant inflows led to *fewer* terrorist attacks. Dreher et al. (2020): larger foreign populations associated with *more* terrorist activity. Forrester et al. (2019, 170 countries): *no evidence* immigration leads to more terrorism. These three published studies reach contradictory conclusions. **No empirical consensus exists** on whether immigration increases terrorism risk in destination countries.

### 3.7 Perception vs. Reality Gap

**Finding:** Immigration is **positively associated with fear of crime** among native-born residents but **did not affect actual crime victimisation rates**. The fear effect correlates with unfavourable attitudes toward immigrants generally — suggesting fear is driven by cultural anxiety rather than experienced victimisation.
- **Source:** Nunziata — *Journal of Population Economics*, Vol. 28(3), pp. 697–736 (2015). Western European countries; ESS + LFS data; individual-level victimisation matched with regional immigration rates; fully controlled.
- **URL:** https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Crime-Perception-and-Victimization-in-Europe...Nunziata/30f087e5b720f64ef91fd57dd0f7d64c1454424b
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — peer-reviewed; controlled; cross-national

**Finding:** Worries about immigrant crime are more pronounced among politically right-wing individuals and in societies with larger non-European populations, but are **unrelated to prior personal victimisation** and **unrelated to the country's actual homicide rate**.
- **Source:** *International Criminology* (Springer, 2025)
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s43576-025-00169-1
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — published 2025; methodology not fully reviewed at time of research

## Counter-Evidence & Debates

**Against "no effect on crime":** The property crime findings in Germany (Gehrsitz & Ungerer; Dehos) are real and methodologically sound — they show modest increases in drug and property crime among asylum seeker populations. These findings should not be dismissed. The correct statement is that violent crime is not significantly increased; property crime shows modest increases among specific, high-vulnerability subgroups in specific conditions (restricted labour market access, large reception centres).

**Against "controls explain everything":** The Piopiunik & Ruhose (2017) study found crime effects even among a group (ethnic German repatriates) that is legally better positioned than asylum seekers. Their finding that local unemployment and pre-existing crime levels moderate the effect is important: immigration-related crime increases are not fixed; they are conditional on local absorptive capacity.

**Against dismissing raw statistics:** The raw PKS and BRÅ data are real measurements of real criminal justice outcomes. That demographic confounders explain much of the gap does not mean the gap is fabricated or unimportant — it means the causal attribution matters. A high crime rate among young, poor, urban men does not become less concerning because those men happen to be foreign-born; the policy question is how to address the underlying deprivation.

## Confounding Factors

- **Age/sex:** The single largest confounder. Migrant populations in most EU countries skew younger and more male than the native population — two powerful independent predictors of crime propensity. Studies that do not control for this produce dramatically inflated apparent effects.
- **Poverty/socioeconomic status:** Concentrated urban poverty is independently associated with higher crime rates. Migrants are disproportionately poor; controlling for poverty removes much of the residual gap.
- **Legal status:** The most powerful *individual-level* predictor of immigrant crime propensity. Undocumented status increases crime; legalisation reduces it (~50% drop in Italian studies).
- **Labour market access:** The mechanism behind the legal-status effect. Migrants with formal employment have a higher opportunity cost of criminal activity.
- **Geographic concentration:** Crime effects are often localised around large reception centres or concentrated settlement areas. National averages understate local effects in specific high-concentration areas.
- **Reporting bias:** Migrants may be more visible to police in certain contexts (e.g., street-level policing in poor urban areas) and therefore more likely to be stopped, questioned, and recorded as suspects.

## Evidence Gaps

1. **EU-wide sexual assault perpetrator data by immigration status** — Not compiled by Eurostat. Country-level data is inconsistent in what it records. This is a genuine measurement gap.
2. **Updated BRÅ crime-by-origin report (Sweden)** — The 2005 report is the last comprehensive demographic-controlled analysis. The Swedish government declined to update it in 2017. Post-2015 changes in Sweden's migrant composition are not captured.
3. **Organised crime and migration** — The intersection of migration routes with organised crime networks (people smuggling, drug trafficking) is documented by Europol but not well measured in academic studies with causal identification.
4. **Second-generation crime trajectories** — Studies focus primarily on first-generation migrants. The crime propensity of second-generation individuals (who experience different integration outcomes) is not well-measured at EU level.
5. **Long-run legal status effects** — Italian studies cover 5–10 years post-legalisation. Whether the crime-reducing effect persists through full labour market integration (15–20 years) is not established.

## Bottom Line

The causal evidence base — natural experiments and quasi-experimental designs across Italy, Germany, Sweden, and the UK — consistently finds that **immigration does not significantly increase violent crime rates** when demographic controls are applied. The raw national statistics (PKS, BRÅ, CBS) that show overrepresentation are real data but are not controlled for age, sex, and socioeconomic status, which together explain the majority of the gap.

**What the evidence does support:**
- Modest increases in property crime and drug offences among specific subgroups (asylum seekers near large reception centres; recognised refugees in Germany) — primarily linked to restricted labour market access
- A perception-reality gap: fear of immigrant crime is not correlated with actual victimisation experience
- Legal status as the critical lever: legalisation reduces recidivism ~50% — the single most evidence-backed policy finding in this domain
- Migrants as high-risk crime *victims*, particularly of hate crime, sexual violence, and exploitation — consistently underemphasised in public debate

**What the evidence does not support:**
- The claim that immigration has no effect on crime at all (property crime effects are documented)
- The claim that raw overrepresentation statistics represent the causal effect of immigration on crime (they don't — they are confounded)
- A causal link from immigration to violent crime increases in EU countries (not found in properly identified studies)

**Confidence level:** High on the null violent crime result (multiple independent natural experiments). High on the legal status / labour market access mechanism (replicated across countries). Moderate on the modest property crime finding (Germany-specific; context-dependent). Low on terrorism causal attributions (contradictory large-N studies). Low on sexual assault perpetrator attribution at EU level (data gap severe).
