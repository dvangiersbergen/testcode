# Domain 1: Fiscal & Economic Impact

**Research date:** 2026-02-22
**Evidence covers:** Net fiscal contributions, GDP effects, welfare usage, entrepreneurship, refugee costs
**Primary sources:** IMF, OECD, Eurostat, EC JRC, peer-reviewed journals (Economic Journal, Fiscal Studies, International Tax and Public Finance)

---

## Evidence Summary

The best available evidence from multiple methodologically rigorous studies finds that migrants are, on average, a small net positive fiscal contributor compared to native-born populations across EU countries — but this average conceals critical disaggregation. Labour migrants (especially intra-EU) are consistently net positive. Refugees and asylum seekers impose net fiscal costs in the short to medium term, which diminish as labour market integration improves. Treating "migrants" as a homogeneous category produces the most misleading fiscal estimates; migration motive and skill level are the decisive variables. GDP effects are positive on the whole (immigration is estimated to raise EU potential output by ~0.5% by 2030), but GDP-per-capita effects depend on whether migrants permanently underperform natives in productivity. Entrepreneurship rates among migrants are equal to or higher than natives. Welfare usage by migrants is lower than natives when properly controlled for age and family structure, with an exception in Nordic-style welfare states for certain subgroups.

## Key Findings

### 1.1 Net Fiscal Position — EU-Wide

**Finding:** Across 15 EU countries 2007–2018, both intra-EU and extra-EU migrants show a *less negative* net fiscal position than native-born populations. Native-born individuals average −€1,545/year net; intra-EU migrants −€469; extra-EU migrants −€493. The native fiscal burden is driven primarily by pension costs; the migrant deficit (where it exists) is driven by unemployment and social assistance.
- **Source:** Boffi, Suari-Andreu, van Vliet — *"The Net Fiscal Position of Migrants in Europe"* (TransEuroWorkS WP 2024; published in *International Tax and Public Finance*, Springer, 2025)
- **Methodology:** Static bottom-up accounting; EU-SILC microdata; OLS regression controlling for individual characteristics; 15 EU countries
- **URLs:** https://transeuroworks.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/The-net-fiscal-position-of-migrants-in-Europe_WP.pdf | https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10797-025-09942-4
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — multi-country, peer-reviewed, replicated in independent studies

**Finding:** Across 23 EU member states in 2015 (EUROMOD microsimulation), migrants contributed on average **€250/year more than natives** to the welfare state. All three groups — natives, intra-EU migrants, extra-EU migrants — are net fiscal recipients; migrants are simply *less negative*. By 2035, the ageing of the native population is projected to reverse the position further in migrants' relative favour.
- **Source:** Christl, Bélanger, Conte, Mazza, Narazani — *"Projecting the Fiscal Impact of Immigration in the EU"* (*Fiscal Studies*, Wiley, 2022, vol. 43(4), pp. 365–385)
- **Methodology:** EUROMOD tax-benefit microsimulation + EU-SILC; 23 EU member states; 6 policy scenarios; projects to 2035
- **URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1475-5890.12314
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — largest multi-country microsimulation study of EU fiscal impact; peer-reviewed

**Finding:** Across the EU 2014–2018, migrants contributed approximately **€1,500 more per capita per year** than native-born. The fiscal impact of migration is "very small in GDP terms, approximately zero on average." Where migrants underperform fiscally, it is driven by lower tax payments, not higher benefit receipts. Age is the single biggest determinant of cross-country variation.
- **Source:** Fiorio, Davoine, Narazani et al. — *"Migration and Public Finances in the EU"* (*International Tax and Public Finance*, Springer, 2023)
- **Methodology:** Bottom-up accounting; EU-SILC microdata; income taxes, cash benefits, indirect taxes, in-kind benefits
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10797-023-09787-9
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — peer-reviewed; consistent with Boffi and Christl findings

**Finding (UK):** EEA migrants made a positive fiscal contribution throughout 1995–2011, even during budget deficit periods. Post-2000 immigrants made a positive contribution regardless of origin. A8 migrants (post-2004 EU accession countries) contributed 10% more than UK-born citizens in direct and indirect taxes and claimed fewer benefits.
- **Source:** Dustmann & Frattini — *"The Fiscal Effects of Immigration to the UK"* (*Economic Journal*, vol. 124, F593–F643, 2014)
- **Methodology:** Individual-level tax/benefit assignment; distinguishes EEA, A8, non-EEA migrants across multiple years
- **URL:** https://academic.oup.com/ej/article-abstract/124/580/F593/5076967
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — highly cited; Rowthorn (2014) critique noted below

**Key methodological note:** Intra-EU migrants are **net positive in all EU welfare regime types** (Nordic, Continental, Mediterranean, Eastern European). The effect is particularly strong in Southern Europe where social expenditure is oriented toward retirement rather than working-age transfers (Österman, Ruist et al., 2023).

### 1.2 Refugee and Asylum Seeker Fiscal Costs

**Finding (Sweden 2007):** Net fiscal redistribution from non-refugee to refugee population = **~1.0% of Swedish GDP**. Four-fifths of this redistribution comes from *lower public revenues* (lower employment rates), not higher spending. Refugees accounted for **55% of social assistance spending** despite being 5.1% of the population. Employment rate gap: 20 percentage points below the adult average. Ruist's own assessment: "clearly noticeable, yet also clearly manageable."
- **Source:** Ruist — *"The Fiscal Cost of Refugee Immigration: The Example of Sweden"* (*Population and Development Review*, vol. 41, 2015, pp. 567–581)
- **Methodology:** First direct estimate of fiscal redistribution specifically to refugee immigrants in a Western country; Swedish registry data
- **URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1728-4457.2015.00085.x
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — registry data; first rigorous refugee-specific study; widely cited and not methodologically challenged

**Finding (Sweden lifetime projection):** Average annual fiscal net contribution per refugee: **−12% to −22% of GDP per capita** depending on origin country's historical labour market integration rate. Lifetime cumulative cost per refugee (58-year horizon): varies accordingly. If the EU received all refugees currently in Asia and Africa, the implied average annual fiscal cost would be at most **0.6% of EU GDP**; spread across all rich countries, each country's cost would be even lower.
- **Source:** Ruist — *"The Fiscal Lifetime Cost of Receiving Refugees"* (CReAM Discussion Paper, 2019)
- **URL:** https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/295593/1/cream-dp1902.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — lifetime projections have wide uncertainty bands; integration-rate assumptions drive results

**Finding (Germany 2023):** Federal-level refugee-related expenditure: **~€29.7 billion in 2023** (up from €21.6 billion in 2021, driven by Ukraine arrivals). Largest cost items: social transfer payments post-asylum (€11.8 billion) and development aid. World Bank (2023): 54% of refugees in Germany found employment within 6 years, rising to 62% after 7 years; 70% in skilled roles, but 41% working below their qualification level; large gender gap (67% men vs. 23% women employed).
- **Sources:** German Federal Government (Statista); ifo Institute (2023); World Bank (2023)
- **URLs:** https://www.ifo.de/en/press-release/2023-10-11/integration-asylum-seekers-into-german-labor-market | https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/d3bf052f21b05b8b5f87f38b921dfd7e-0050062023/original/WDR-German-case-study-FINAL.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on cost figures (administrative data); 🟫 Moderate on employment trajectory (administrative + survey data)

### 1.3 GDP and GDP-Per-Capita Effects

**Finding:** The 2020–2023 EU migration surge (net ~2 million workers) is estimated to raise EU potential output by **0.5% by 2030** (range: 0.2–0.7%). This is "slightly less than half of the euro area's annual potential GDP growth." In 2022–23, approximately **two-thirds of all newly created EU jobs** were filled by non-EU citizens, while EU-citizen unemployment remained at historic lows. Initial fiscal costs: ~0.2% of EU GDP; rising to ~1% in countries with largest per-capita refugee inflows. Migration was modestly inflationary (+0.1 pp) in the short run.
- **Source:** IMF Working Paper WP/24/211, Caselli, Lin, Toscani, Yao (September 2024)
- **Methodology:** General equilibrium modelling; immigrant productivity assumed 20% below natives as lower bound
- **URL:** https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2024/09/27/migration-into-the-eu-stocktaking-of-recent-developments-and-macroeconomic-implications-555578
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — GE modelling; results are scenario-dependent; productivity assumption is key driver

**Finding:** A direct positive relationship exists between working-age immigrants and GDP per capita across all 27 EU countries, 2008–2020. The effect is stronger for female immigrants than male immigrants.
- **Source:** *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health* (PMC/MDPI, 2023)
- **Methodology:** Panel data econometric models; 27 EU countries; Eurostat data
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9819786/
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — panel OLS; limited causal identification; consistent with other studies

**Finding (refugee-specific):** Refugee immigration yields **large initial negative effects** on GDP per capita and employment rates, with positive long-term effects that are substantially larger than the initial negative effects. Growth is higher when refugee immigrants are relatively young and sufficiently qualified.
- **Source:** *Intereconomics* (2021) — Germany case study; IMF WP/23/259 (2023) — cross-country
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — dependent on modelling assumptions about integration rates

**Key caveat on GDP per capita:** All GDP growth from migration is positive. GDP *per capita* effects depend on whether migrants permanently underperform natives in productivity. If a persistent productivity gap exists (IMF WP/24/211 assumes 20% lower), per-capita effects are initially negative but fade as capital adjusts. If migrants converge to native productivity, per-capita effects are positive throughout. The empirical record on convergence is mixed and origin-group-dependent.

### 1.4 Welfare Usage vs. Native-Born Populations

Summary of controlled studies:

| Study | Country/Region | Key finding (controlled for age/SES) |
|---|---|---|
| Huber & Oberdabernig (2016) | 16 EU countries | Immigrants receive *less* social benefits than natives overall |
| Christl et al. (2022) | 23 EU countries | Migrants less negative fiscal position; native burden driven by pensions |
| Boffi et al. (2024) | 15 EU countries | Migrants net fiscal recipients to a lesser degree than natives |
| EC Knowledge4Policy (2019) | EU-wide | Observable demographic differences largely explain raw welfare gap |
| PMC longitudinal study (2022) | Germany | Welfare dependency *declines* with duration of stay; assimilation evident |
| Boeri (2010) | Nordic states | In generous welfare states, residual dependency persists for certain subgroups after full controls |

**Key nuance:** Raw welfare usage data typically shows higher migrant usage. This disappears or reverses in most countries once age, family structure, and employment status are controlled for. The exception is Nordic and generous Continental welfare states, where a residual dependency effect persists among low-skilled recent arrivals. Pensions drive native fiscal costs; unemployment and social assistance drive migrant fiscal costs.
- **Sources:** EC Knowledge4Policy (2019): https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/tr_final_after_last_revision_21052019.pdf | Germany longitudinal: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9672685/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on controlled comparisons — consistent across multiple independent studies

### 1.5 Entrepreneurship Rates

**Finding:** Migrants' share of self-employed in the OECD grew from **11% (2006) to 17% (2022)**. In the EU, foreign-born workers' share of self-employed nearly doubled from <7% (2013) to **12% (2022)**. Self-employment rate in 2022: **13.8% (foreign-born) vs. 13.4% (native-born)**. In two-thirds of OECD countries, immigrants are more likely to be self-employed than native-born. Migrant self-employment created **~4 million jobs (2011–2021)** — 15% employment growth; each migrant self-employed creates ~0.2 jobs on average. In Germany, **60% of unicorn companies** (valued >$1 billion) have at least one immigrant founder.
- **Source:** OECD International Migration Outlook 2024, special chapter on migrant entrepreneurship
- **URL:** https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2024/11/international-migration-outlook-2024_c6f3e803/full-report/migrant-entrepreneurship-in-oecd-countries_72f44494.html
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on self-employment rate data (administrative); 🟫 Moderate on job creation estimates

**Caveat:** Many migrant self-employed engage in "necessity entrepreneurship" — self-employment to bypass labour market barriers, not innovation-driven. Most do not create jobs or innovate; many startups dissolve within a few years. The German unicorn statistic is impressive but atypical.

## Counter-Evidence & Debates

**Rowthorn's critique of Dustmann-Frattini (2014):** Argued the study overestimated migrant earnings and failed to account for job competition effects. Rowthorn's own fiscal impact range for EEA migrants: **−0.47% to +0.23% of GDP** depending on assumptions. He argued that including second-generation children in the native column (as Dustmann-Frattini did) artificially boosts the migrant fiscal advantage. This is the most technically grounded published critique of the major positive-contribution studies. Dustmann-Frattini's response: their methodology is standard and Rowthorn's alternative assumptions are not better supported.

**On "welfare magnet" hypothesis:** Tito Boeri (Bocconi) tested the hypothesis that generous welfare states attract more benefit-seeking migrants. Finding: in Nordic-style welfare states, a residual dependency effect persists for certain immigrant subgroups (low-skilled, recent arrivals) even after full demographic controls. This is a real finding but limited in scope — it does not apply to labour migrants or to most EU welfare systems.

**On GDP per capita:** The standard finding that immigration raises GDP (aggregate) is not seriously contested. The contested question is GDP *per capita*. Immigration adds workers and consumers; whether it raises or lowers income per existing resident depends on capital adjustment speed, productivity gaps, and distributional effects. Most studies find near-zero average per-capita effects, with positive effects for high-skilled natives and negative effects concentrated on low-skilled native workers who compete with immigrants.

## Confounding Factors

- **Migration motive** is the dominant confounder in all fiscal analyses. Studies reporting aggregate "migrant" averages obscure a 3:1 or 4:1 ratio between labour migrant fiscal contribution and asylum seeker fiscal cost.
- **Age at arrival:** Migrants who arrive young (under 35) make the strongest lifetime fiscal contributions. Arrival age has a stronger effect on lifetime net contribution than education level in several studies.
- **Duration of stay:** Fiscal impact is almost always negative in years 1–5 and improves progressively. Point-in-time snapshots during the first 5 years after a refugee surge will show the worst-case fiscal picture.
- **Welfare system generosity:** Residual welfare dependency effects are found mainly in Nordic states. Southern European and Eastern European EU countries show no such pattern.
- **Counting conventions:** Whether second-generation immigrants are counted in the "migrant" or "native" column can shift aggregate fiscal estimates by 0.2–0.4% of GDP. Rowthorn's critique correctly identifies this as an unresolved methodological issue.
- **Infrastructure and public capital:** Most fiscal studies exclude the share of public capital (roads, schools, defence) attributable to migrants. Including these costs produces more negative estimates; excluding them produces more favourable ones.

## Evidence Gaps

1. **Long-run productivity convergence:** The crucial unknown for GDP per capita. If migrants permanently underperform natives by 20%, per-capita effects are modest or negative. If they converge, they are positive. The empirical record is mixed and origin-group dependent. No EU-wide longitudinal study with sufficient duration exists.
2. **Second-generation fiscal contribution modelling:** Most fiscal studies stop at first generation. Second-generation outcomes (narrowing education gap, persistent labour market gap) have not been fully integrated into lifetime fiscal modelling.
3. **Circular/temporary migration fiscal accounting:** Migrants who leave before drawing on pensions make larger net contributions than stayers. The proportion who leave and at what point in the life cycle is poorly measured.
4. **Fiscal impact disaggregated by destination region:** Studies are predominantly national. Local fiscal effects in high-migration cities (Berlin, Amsterdam, Stockholm) vs. rural areas are not well captured at national level.
5. **Post-2022 Ukrainian cohort:** With ~6 million displaced Ukrainians in the EU, this is the largest single cohort in modern EU history. Their fiscal trajectory is not yet measured — employment integration rates vary from >50% (Poland, Lithuania) to <25% (Germany, Austria).

---

## Bottom Line

The aggregate fiscal evidence across the EU is consistent: migrants are, on average, a small net positive contributor to public finances relative to native-born populations. This finding is robust across independent methodologies (EU-SILC bottom-up accounting, EUROMOD microsimulation, UK individual-level data) and multiple research teams. The effect is small in GDP terms — approximately zero at the macro level — and should not be presented as a major fiscal windfall.

**The disaggregated picture is where the policy-relevant evidence lies:**
- Labour migrants (intra-EU and skilled extra-EU): net positive across all metrics
- Asylum seekers and refugees: net negative in the short to medium term; costs are real, measurable (1% of Swedish GDP at peak; ~€30 billion/year in Germany), and decline with integration success
- Family-reunification migrants: consistently the weakest fiscal performers in every country study

GDP effects of migration are positive at the aggregate level. GDP per capita effects are near-zero to slightly positive on average, with distributional effects that benefit high-skilled native workers and impose costs on low-skilled native workers who compete directly with immigrants.

**Confidence level:** High on the directional findings (positive aggregate fiscal contribution; labour vs. asylum disaggregation). Moderate on specific magnitudes, which vary with methodology and country context. Low on long-run per-capita GDP effects, which depend on unresolved productivity convergence questions.
