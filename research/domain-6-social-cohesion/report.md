# Domain 6: Social Cohesion & Integration

**Research date:** 2026-02-22
**Evidence covers:** Language acquisition, labour market integration (1st/2nd generation), educational attainment, intermarriage, residential segregation, social trust (Putnam), cultural values (WVS/ESS), religious practice, second-generation identity, Islamic immigration and cultural effects
**Primary sources:** Eurostat, ESS, WVS, Pew Research, OECD PISA, JRC/D4I, PMC/academic journals

---

## Evidence Summary

Integration outcomes follow a consistent generational pattern across all EU studies: first-generation migrants from non-EU countries (especially MENA and Sub-Saharan Africa) show significant gaps in employment, education, income, social participation, and cultural value convergence relative to native-born populations. Second-generation outcomes are substantially better across all dimensions but a measurable gap persists. Third-generation convergence in cultural values (including attitudes toward gender equality and homosexuality) is documented even for groups that show large first- and second-generation gaps. Origin group is the strongest predictor of integration trajectory: Eastern European EU migrants integrate fastest; MENA and Sub-Saharan African migrants show the widest initial gaps and the slowest convergence, with Turkish-origin populations as a persistent outlier in several European countries. Putnam's diversity-trust hypothesis is not reliably replicated in European contexts; segregation, not diversity per se, is the better predictor of trust decline. Religious practice among migrants (including Muslims) declines across generations but does so more slowly and differently than among native Europeans, with some second-generation Muslim cohorts showing religious revival rather than secularisation.

## Key Findings

### 6.1 Language Acquisition

**Finding:** Language acquisition rates vary by host-country policy, community size, and linguistic distance between origin and destination. In Germany, approximately **75% of refugees** who arrived in 2017 had completed at least one language course; within three years, **40% achieved good or very good proficiency**. Credential recognition combined with language training yielded a **19.8% wage increase** and **24.5 pp employment rate increase** vs. those without recognition.
- **Source:** IPOL Study (2024), European Parliament Research; Eurostat administrative data
- **URL:** https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/STUD/2024/754232/IPOL_STU(2024)754232_EN.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — programme evaluation data; not a controlled trial

**Key determinants of language acquisition:** "Three E's" — Exposure (living environment, interaction with host society), Efficiency (age at arrival, prior education), and Economic incentives (intended duration of stay, earnings potential). Denmark's "work-first" strategy improved short-term employment but produced **lasting negative effects on language acquisition**. Language-first strategies show better long-run integration outcomes.
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — comparative policy analysis; evidence is directional

### 6.2 Labour Market Integration — 1st and 2nd Generation

**Finding (Eurostat 2024 — Tier 1):**
- Non-EU citizen unemployment: fell from **21.4% (2014) to 12.3% (2024)**, but still **2.4× higher** than for nationals (5.1%)
- Second-generation employment rate: **73%** vs. **78%** for natives (5 pp gap — substantially smaller than first generation)
- Non-EU citizens on temporary contracts: **22.5%** vs. **10.9%** for nationals
- **Source:** Eurostat Labour Market Indicators (2024)
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Migrant_integration_statistics_%E2%80%93_labour_market_indicators
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — Tier 1 administrative data

**Finding — wage gap (Belgium matched employer-employee data):** First-generation immigrants from developing countries earn significantly less than natives after controlling for human capital and sector. **Second-generation immigrants show no statistically significant adjusted wage gap** when firm fixed effects are included — the ethnic wage penalty disappears in the second generation in Belgium.
- **Source:** *Journal of Economic Inequality* / Springer (2024–2025); Belgian matched data 1999–2016, 1.1 million workers
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10888-024-09663-4
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on Belgium; 🟫 Moderate for EU generalisation

**Finding — intergenerational mobility:** Large income gaps for first-generation immigrants diminish substantially in the second generation across most of 15 countries studied. Sons of immigrants show **lower mobility than daughters**, indicating a gender-specific integration barrier. First-generation migrants generally experience more downward than upward mobility; in the second generation, upward mobility is more prevalent.
- **Source:** CEPR VoxEU, *"Intergenerational Mobility of Immigrants in 15 Destination Countries"*; INET Oxford WP No. 2026-02 (*"Intergenerational Social Mobility of Migrants in 22 European Countries"*, EU-SILC 2019)
- **URLs:** https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/intergenerational-mobility-immigrants-15-destination-countries | https://www.inet.ox.ac.uk/publications/no-2026-02-the-intergenerational-social-mobility-of-migrants-in-22-european-countries
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — cross-country comparisons with varying methodologies; directional finding consistent

**Finding — job quality (first generation):** First-generation migrants — especially women and non-EU nationals — are disproportionately concentrated in physically demanding, low-quality jobs. This disadvantage persists into but does not fully transfer to the second generation.
- **Source:** *De Economist* / Springer (2025); EU Labour Force Survey
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10645-025-09453-x
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — LFS-based; selection effects not fully controlled

### 6.3 Educational Attainment by Generation

**Finding (Eurostat 2024 — Tier 1):**
- Young non-EU citizens are **3× more likely** to drop out of education early: **25.3% vs. 8.2%** for nationals
- Early school leaving rate for young non-EU men: **28.2%** vs. **9.8%** for national men (2023)
- Tertiary attainment (ages 25–34): nationals **45.1%**, non-EU citizens **36.7%**
- Native-born individuals with one foreign-born parent have the **highest tertiary rates** in some countries (e.g. Cyprus, Spain)
- **Source:** Eurostat Migrant Integration Statistics — Education (2024) — Tier 1
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Migrant_integration_statistics_-_education
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong

**Finding — PISA trends (2006–2022):** The immigrant-native academic achievement gap **increased** over this period in EU countries, even as the same gap narrowed in English-speaking immigration countries (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Singapore). EU educational systems are systematically less effective at equalising outcomes for immigrant-background students than their English-speaking counterparts.
- **Source:** *Learning and Instruction* / ScienceDirect (2025) — six waves of PISA data 2006–2022
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191491X25001130
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — six PISA waves; comparative design; peer-reviewed

**Finding — second-generation:** Second-generation students significantly outperform first-generation but still show measurable gap vs. native-born peers in most EU countries. Parental education *relative to host society* (not absolute) is the key predictor — parents educated abroad but underemployed still transmit educational aspirations.
- **Source:** OECD PISA 2022; *Social Science Research* / ScienceDirect (2024)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on PISA pattern; 🟫 Moderate on mechanism

### 6.4 Intermarriage Rates

**Finding:** Intermarriage rates vary substantially by country and origin group. France: ~27% of individuals born in France to immigrant parents married non-migrant partners (early 2010s). Germany: ~16% of Turkish-background individuals married non-migrant partners (BAMF, 2011). UK and France show higher intermarriage rates than Hungary and Bulgaria. Second-generation individuals have significantly higher intermarriage rates than first-generation across all studies.
- **Source:** *Nationalities Papers* / Cambridge Core; Lanzieri (Academia.edu)
- **URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/nationalities-papers/article/dynamics-of-intermarriage-in-europe/841972E4699946B77B1BC80F0BFE0279
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak — no consistent EU-wide time series; no official EU integration indicator includes intermarriage (Lanzieri notes this as a measurement gap)

### 6.5 Residential Segregation

**Finding:** Non-EU migrants are more residentially segregated than EU migrants in almost every EU country studied. Belgium (Brussels, Antwerp, Liège) shows the strongest spatial isolation of ethnically deprived groups; non-EU migrant neighbourhoods correlate with lowest employment, income, and tertiary education rates. Sweden shows increasing clustering in already-concentrated neighbourhoods. Germany is relatively low nationally; Ruhr cities above average. Southern EU (Italy, Spain) shows lower dissimilarity indices than Northern/Western Europe. Socioeconomic inequality plays as large a role as ethnicity in shaping residential patterns — distinct from the US where race alone drives segregation.
- **Sources:** EU JRC D4I Report (JRC115159) — 2011 Census, 8 EU member states; Benassi et al. (2020) *Spatial Demography*; Benassi et al. (2023) *International Migration*
- **URLs:** https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC115159 | https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40980-020-00064-5
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on patterns; 🟫 Moderate on causal mechanisms (socioeconomic vs. ethnic sorting)

### 6.6 Social Trust — Putnam's Research and EU Replications

**Putnam's original finding (USA, 2007):** In the most diverse US communities, neighbours trust one another about **half as much** as in the most homogeneous communities. All civic health indicators are lower in diverse settings. In the short run, diversity reduces social trust and civic participation — even within one's own group ("hunkering down").
- **Source:** Putnam — *"E Pluribus Unum"*, *Scandinavian Political Studies*, 30(2), 137–174 (2007)
- **URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9477.2007.00176.x
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the original US finding; 41 communities, N > 29,000

**EU replication attempts — mixed results:**
- **Cross-national Europe (Gesthuizen et al.):** Putnam's hypothesis "must be refuted" in European societies. Economic inequality and democratic history are more important than diversity.
- **Netherlands (Lanceé & Dronkers, 2008):** Partial replication — ethnic diversity reduces trust in neighbours for both immigrants and natives.
- **Netherlands neighbourhoods (Gijsberts et al.):** Only **frequency of neighbourhood contact** (not broader trust) is significantly reduced by diversity.
- **Norway, Sweden, UK:** No significant relationship between diversity and general trust.
- **PMC (2023) — key resolution:** The diversity-trust relationship is **mediated by segregation at the wider community level**. Diverse neighbourhoods within *integrated wider communities* show no trust penalty. Diverse neighbourhoods within *segregated wider communities* show significant trust decline.
- **Sources:** Multiple; key synthesis at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9887315/
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate on EU replications — context-dependent; segregation is the key moderator, not diversity per se

**Assessment:** The Putnam diversity-trust effect is stronger where segregation, income inequality, and low institutional trust coincide. It is absent or weak in Nordic countries and at the cross-national EU level. Policy implication: addressing segregation matters more than managing diversity per se.

### 6.7 Cultural Value Surveys (WVS / ESS)

**Finding (ESS 2025 — key study):** Value assimilation by immigrants has only a **modest effect** on their integration outcomes across occupational status, social participation, and political participation. Conservation values are the exception — holding more conservative values hinders political participation. Civic integration programmes that assume value adoption will improve integration outcomes are **not well-supported** by this evidence.
- **Source:** Suárez & Hannikainen — *"Integration Gaps Persist Despite Immigrants' Value Assimilation"*, ESS rounds 1–10 (2002–2020), 28 EU countries, N = 261,830; *Frontiers in Sociology* / PMC (2025)
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11997842/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — large N; multiple ESS rounds; peer-reviewed

**Finding (ESS attitude trends):** European acceptance of immigrants has shifted slightly downward since 2020: acceptance of immigrants from poorer non-European countries fell from **62.5% to 59.0%** (2020–2023). Young people under 25 are consistently more accepting; recent decline is driven by over-25s.
- **Source:** ESS Round 11 analysis (RFBerlin, 2023); ESS Round 11 data now includes Estonia and Ukraine
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — survey data; attitude-behaviour gap acknowledged

### 6.8 Religious Practice and Secularisation

**Finding:** First-generation migrants are markedly more religious than native Europeans across all religious affiliations. Second-generation Muslims retain higher religiosity than native peers but are more secularised than their parents. Religious practices **increase in the first years after arrival** (dislocation, community-seeking), then gradually decline with longer residence. Employment and liberal attitudes on gender and sexuality are associated with faster secularisation — a pattern **similar for Muslim and Christian newcomers**.
- **Source:** PMC / *European Journal of Population* (2023) — pooled ESS data, Western Europe 2002–2018; Springer *KZfSS* (2023) — Netherlands study
- **URLs:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036697/ | https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0037768620948478
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — multiple country studies; pooled ESS data; consistent pattern

**Finding — Islamic identity in second generation:** Second-generation Muslims increasingly reconceptualise Islam in **more private, spiritual, and symbolic terms** — less about outward practice, more about personal identity. Norwegian Muslim youth show religious revival — religion replaces ethnicity as primary identity marker. Second-generation Protestants show evidence of religious revival too, suggesting this may reflect minority-identity dynamics, not Islam specifically.
- **Sources:** *Sociology of Religion* (Friberg & Sterri 2021; Kasselstrand & Mahmoudi 2020); ScienceDirect (2022)
- **URLs:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0197918320986767 | https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0147176722001171
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — qualitative and survey data; Scandinavian context may not generalise

### 6.9 Integration Outcomes by Origin Group

The most comprehensive comparative study (ESS rounds 7 & 8, Western Europe):

| Origin group | Structural integration | Cultural value convergence | Generational trajectory |
|---|---|---|---|
| West European / North American | High | Most liberal (at majority level) | No gap |
| Eastern / SE European | Moderate | Less liberal on some indicators | Fast convergence |
| Middle Eastern / N African / Central Asian | Low (all dimensions) | Least liberal | Convergence by 3rd generation |
| Sub-Saharan African | Low (all dimensions) | Less liberal | Convergence by 3rd generation |
| South / Southeast Asian | Moderate-low | Less liberal | Moderate convergence |

**Key finding:** "All groups converge with the majority in the second or third generation, leaving no third-generation groups which are significantly less liberal than their autochthonous peers." However, convergence takes 2–3 generations for MENA and Sub-Saharan African groups — not 1.
- **Source:** PMC / *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* (2021) — ESS rounds 7 & 8, pooled, Western Europe
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8116888/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — ESS data; multi-country; peer-reviewed

**MENA educational heterogeneity:** Within the MENA category, educational composition varies enormously — Syrian, Lebanese, and Palestinian migrants tend to arrive highly qualified; Moroccan, Algerian, Tunisian, and Turkish migrants are disproportionately less educated. Treating MENA as a monolith produces misleading averages.

**Eastern European specificity:** EU freedom of movement enables high return and circular migration rates, reducing long-term integration pressure. Lower cultural distance from West European norms on gender and sexuality produces faster value convergence.

**France specificity:** Intergenerational mobility is particularly low for people of North African, Sub-Saharan African, and Turkish descent — these groups remain the most disadvantaged in the French labour market even in the second generation (CEPR VoxEU).

### 6.10 Islamic Immigration: Cultural and Traditional Effects

This sub-section covers empirically documented effects of Islamic immigration specifically on cultural norms and practices of EU receiving societies. Political commentary is excluded; only survey data and academic research is cited.

#### 6.10a Cultural Value Differences

**WVS placement:** Islamic Middle Eastern societies cluster in the strongest traditional/survival values quadrant in WVS cross-national mapping. Protestant Northern Europe clusters in the strongest secular-rational/self-expression values quadrant. These represent the largest value-distance pair on the WVS cultural map.

**Key ESS finding (Ruud Koopmans, WZB Berlin):** Koopmans' 2015 study (*International Journal of Comparative Sociology*) using ESS data on 6 EU countries found that **44% of Muslims** agreed that Jews cannot be trusted, compared to 13% of Christians. 65% said there is only one interpretation of Islam and all Muslims must follow it. 75% thought that religious rules are more important than state laws. These findings are the most-cited — and most disputed — in the European Islam integration literature.
- **Source:** Koopmans (2015), *International Journal of Comparative Sociology* — Tier 2
- **Key critique:** Sample sizes per country were small; the six-country sample may not represent European Muslims broadly; data now 15+ years old. Koopmans responded to critiques by noting the consistency of findings across independent surveys.
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — peer-reviewed but contested; replicated partially by subsequent surveys, not fully

**FRA EU Minorities Survey (EU-MIDIS II, 2017 — Tier 1):** Covering discrimination experiences of Muslims, Roma, Jews, Africans, and other groups in EU member states. Finds high rates of discrimination against Muslims in employment and public life — confirming structural barriers are real.
- **Source:** EU Fundamental Rights Agency, EU-MIDIS II (2017)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on discrimination data

#### 6.10b Gender Norms

**Finding:** Female labour force participation (FLFP) among Muslim-majority-origin women is systematically lower than among native EU women and other migrant groups. This gap partially persists into the second generation but narrows substantially. Country-level variation is large: Turkish-origin women in Germany have significantly higher FLFP than Moroccan-origin women in the Netherlands, suggesting host-country institutional context matters.
- **Source:** OECD *"Settling In 2023"*; Eurostat Labour Force Survey (Tier 1)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the FLFP gap (Tier 1 data); 🟫 Moderate on causal mechanism

**Finding — honour violence and FGM:** Consistent quantitative data on honour-based violence in EU countries is not available at EU level. Country-level data exists in the Netherlands (Dutch police records), Germany (BKA), and UK (Home Office). Eurostat does not compile EU-wide honour violence statistics. FGM prevalence estimates for EU exist (EIGE, 2013 and 2015) covering girls at risk — estimates range from ~180,000 (UK) to ~65,000 (France) to ~45,000 (Germany) as of 2012 data, predominantly from Sub-Saharan Africa and to a lesser extent from MENA. These are estimates of "girls at risk," not confirmed cases.
- **Source:** EIGE — *"Estimation of Girls at Risk of Female Genital Mutilation in the European Union"* (2015)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak quantitatively — estimates, not registry data; significant methodological uncertainty

#### 6.10c Antisemitism

**Finding:** Multiple surveys document elevated antisemitic attitudes among Muslim-background populations in Europe compared to the general population and to Christian-background populations.
- FRA survey (2018): 63% of Jews in Germany, France, and Belgium said antisemitism was expressed "often or always" by "someone with a Muslim extremist view."
- Kantor Center Antisemitism Report (2024): Documents increase in antisemitic incidents post-October 7, 2023, with data on perpetrator backgrounds where available.
- **Key methodological note:** Attribution data for antisemitic incidents to specific perpetrator backgrounds is inconsistent across EU countries. Some countries record perpetrator religion/background; most do not.
- **Source:** FRA — *"Experiences and Perceptions of Antisemitism"* (2018); Kantor Center (2024) — Tier 3 (Kantor Center is research-oriented)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate on survey data; 🟧 Weak on incident attribution data

#### 6.10d Freedom of Expression

**Finding:** Survey data shows Muslim-background populations in Europe have higher support for restrictions on speech criticising Islam than the general population. Specific figures vary by survey and country. Pew Research (2006) found substantial minorities in European Muslim populations (30–35% in France, UK, Germany) supported introduction of Sharia law. More recent Pew data (2017) found lower support for Sharia as official law in Western European Muslim communities (8–12% in Germany and France), though support for "Islamic courts for family/property disputes" was higher.
- **Source:** Pew Research Center *"European Muslims: Views on Sharia"* (2017) — Tier 3/4
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak — survey wording effects significant; Pew samples may not capture the most conservative or isolated subgroups

#### 6.10e Third-Generation Convergence

The ESS multi-wave study (Suárez & Hannikainen 2025, N = 261,830) cited in section 6.7 finds that all migrant groups — including Muslim-origin populations — show value convergence toward host-country norms across generations. Third-generation individuals show no statistically significant difference in "secular-rational" values from autochthonous peers. **This is the most important counter-finding to cultural incompatibility claims.** The convergence takes 2–3 generations for MENA populations, longer than for Eastern European populations.

## Counter-Evidence & Debates

**Against Putnam:** Cross-national EU evidence does not confirm the diversity-trust relationship. Nordic countries with high diversity and low segregation show no trust decline. The dominant counter-explanation is that segregation (not diversity) drives trust erosion — and that Putnam's US findings reflect specifically American patterns of racial segregation.

**Against cultural incompatibility claims:** Third-generation value convergence is documented across all origin groups, including MENA (ESS 2025 study, N = 261,830). The question is not whether convergence happens but over how many generations. Critics of the convergence thesis note that 2–3 generation timelines involve 60–90 years of cultural transition, which is non-trivial in terms of social policy.

**Against integration pessimism:** The Belgian matched-employer study shows the ethnic wage penalty disappears in the second generation when employer effects are controlled. This suggests **employer discrimination**, not human capital deficiency, drives the first-generation wage gap.

**Against Koopmans:** His 2015 Islamic fundamentalism findings used small per-country samples and data from a single ESS round. The ESS 2025 large-N study found value assimilation is occurring, even if slowly. Critics argue Koopmans conflates conservative religious views with anti-democratic or violent tendencies.

## Confounding Factors

- **Socioeconomic status:** Most measured integration gaps correlate with poverty and low education as much as with migration background. Disentangling ethnicity from class is methodologically difficult.
- **Discrimination:** Belgian employer-employee data shows discrimination accounts for part of the wage gap independent of human capital. Studies that do not control for discrimination understate integration success.
- **Geographic concentration:** Studies of segregated urban neighbourhoods will show worse integration outcomes than national-level analyses. Both levels are valid but should not be conflated.
- **Refugee vs. labour migrant:** Refugees show worse integration outcomes on virtually all dimensions compared to labour migrants of similar origin. Studies that pool these groups overestimate average integration failure.
- **Gender:** Sons of immigrants consistently show worse mobility than daughters in multiple European studies. Male-specific barriers (labour market discrimination, peer effects, identity conflicts) require gender-disaggregated analysis.
- **Religion measurement:** Survey questions about religious belief, practice, and identity capture different dimensions. High practice rates do not necessarily predict cultural or civic incompatibility.

---

## Evidence Gaps

1. **EU-wide intermarriage statistics** — No official EU integration indicator includes intermarriage. Data is country-specific and methodologically inconsistent.
2. **Third-generation data** — Most studies cover first and second generation. True third-generation samples are small in most EU countries. Projected convergence relies on extrapolation.
3. **Honour violence and forced marriage statistics** — Systematically absent at EU level; inconsistent at member-state level.
4. **Long-run religious trajectory** — Whether second-generation Muslim religious revival is a transitional phase or a stable equilibrium is not established. Current data covers at most 30–40 years of post-immigration observation.
5. **Causal mechanisms for integration failure** — Discrimination, residential segregation, school system quality, and cultural distance all contribute to integration gaps, but their relative contributions are not well-identified causally.

## Bottom Line

Integration is generationally progressive but slow for the largest non-EU origin groups. First-generation non-EU migrants (especially MENA and Sub-Saharan African) show significant gaps in employment, education, income, civic participation, and cultural values relative to native-born populations. Second-generation outcomes are substantially better across all dimensions. Third-generation convergence in cultural values — including attitudes toward gender equality and sexual orientation — is empirically documented even for groups with the largest first-generation gaps, but this takes 60–90 years (2–3 generations) for MENA populations.

Social trust is not straightforwardly reduced by diversity. Segregation, not diversity, is the key moderating variable. Putnam's US findings do not reliably replicate in European contexts.

Value assimilation occurs but is a weak predictor of integration outcomes. Labour market access, language training with credential recognition, and anti-discrimination enforcement have stronger empirical support as integration levers.

Islamic immigration introduces the largest average cultural value distance of any major migrant group (WVS mapping). This is documented in survey data. The convergence evidence shows this distance narrows but does not disappear until the third generation. The policy-relevant question is how to manage a 60–90 year transition period — that is a normative question beyond the scope of this empirical review.

**Confidence level:** High on employment/education gap data (Tier 1 Eurostat). High on generational improvement pattern (multiple independent studies). Moderate on value convergence trajectory (ESS multi-wave, good N, but extrapolation to 3rd generation). Moderate on Putnam EU replications (context-dependent). Low on honour violence, FGM, and informal religious practice data (measurement gaps severe).
