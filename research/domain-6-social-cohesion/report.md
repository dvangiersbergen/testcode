# Domain 6: Social Cohesion & Integration

**Research date:** 2026-02-22
**Evidence covers:** Language acquisition, labour market integration (1st/2nd generation), educational attainment, intermarriage, residential segregation, social trust (Putnam), cultural values (WVS/ESS), religious practice, second-generation identity, Islamic immigration and cultural effects
**Primary sources:** Eurostat, ESS, WVS, Pew Research, OECD PISA, JRC/D4I, PMC/academic journals

---

## Evidence Summary

Integration outcomes follow a consistent generational pattern across all EU studies: first-generation migrants from non-EU countries (especially MENA and Sub-Saharan Africa) show significant gaps in employment, education, income, social participation, and cultural value convergence relative to native-born populations. Second-generation outcomes are substantially better across all dimensions but a measurable gap persists. Third-generation convergence in cultural values (including attitudes toward gender equality and homosexuality) is documented even for groups that show large first- and second-generation gaps. Origin group is the strongest predictor of integration trajectory: Eastern European EU migrants integrate fastest; MENA and Sub-Saharan African migrants show the widest initial gaps and the slowest convergence, with Turkish-origin populations as a persistent outlier in several European countries. Putnam's diversity-trust hypothesis is not reliably replicated in European contexts; segregation, not diversity per se, is the better predictor of trust decline. Religious practice among migrants (including Muslims) declines across generations but does so more slowly and differently than among native Europeans, with some second-generation Muslim cohorts showing religious revival rather than secularisation.

## Key Findings

### 6.1 Language Acquisition

**Finding:** Language acquisition rates vary by host-country policy, community size, and linguistic distance between origin and destination. In Germany, approximately **75% of refugees** who arrived in 2017 had completed at least one language course; within three years, **40% achieved good or very good proficiency**. Credential recognition combined with language training yielded a **19.8% wage increase** and **24.5 pp employment rate increase** vs. those without recognition.
- **Source:** IPOL Study (2024), European Parliament Research; Eurostat administrative data
- **URL:** https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/STUD/2024/754232/IPOL_STU(2024)754232_EN.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — programme evaluation data; not a controlled trial

**Key determinants of language acquisition:** "Three E's" — Exposure (living environment, interaction with host society), Efficiency (age at arrival, prior education), and Economic incentives (intended duration of stay, earnings potential). Denmark's "work-first" strategy improved short-term employment but produced **lasting negative effects on language acquisition**. Language-first strategies show better long-run integration outcomes.
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — comparative policy analysis; evidence is directional

### 6.2 Labour Market Integration — 1st and 2nd Generation

**Finding (Eurostat 2024 — Tier 1):**
- Non-EU citizen unemployment: fell from **21.4% (2014) to 12.3% (2024)**, but still **2.4× higher** than for nationals (5.1%)
- Second-generation employment rate: **73%** vs. **78%** for natives (5 pp gap — substantially smaller than first generation)
- Non-EU citizens on temporary contracts: **22.5%** vs. **10.9%** for nationals
- **Source:** Eurostat Labour Market Indicators (2024)
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Migrant_integration_statistics_%E2%80%93_labour_market_indicators
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — Tier 1 administrative data

**Finding — wage gap (Belgium matched employer-employee data):** First-generation immigrants from developing countries earn significantly less than natives after controlling for human capital and sector. **Second-generation immigrants show no statistically significant adjusted wage gap** when firm fixed effects are included — the ethnic wage penalty disappears in the second generation in Belgium.
- **Source:** *Journal of Economic Inequality* / Springer (2024–2025); Belgian matched data 1999–2016, 1.1 million workers
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10888-024-09663-4
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on Belgium; 🟫 Moderate for EU generalisation

**Finding — intergenerational mobility:** Large income gaps for first-generation immigrants diminish substantially in the second generation across most of 15 countries studied. Sons of immigrants show **lower mobility than daughters**, indicating a gender-specific integration barrier. First-generation migrants generally experience more downward than upward mobility; in the second generation, upward mobility is more prevalent.
- **Source:** CEPR VoxEU, *"Intergenerational Mobility of Immigrants in 15 Destination Countries"*; INET Oxford WP No. 2026-02 (*"Intergenerational Social Mobility of Migrants in 22 European Countries"*, EU-SILC 2019)
- **URLs:** https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/intergenerational-mobility-immigrants-15-destination-countries | https://www.inet.ox.ac.uk/publications/no-2026-02-the-intergenerational-social-mobility-of-migrants-in-22-european-countries
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — cross-country comparisons with varying methodologies; directional finding consistent

**Finding — job quality (first generation):** First-generation migrants — especially women and non-EU nationals — are disproportionately concentrated in physically demanding, low-quality jobs. This disadvantage persists into but does not fully transfer to the second generation.
- **Source:** *De Economist* / Springer (2025); EU Labour Force Survey
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10645-025-09453-x
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — LFS-based; selection effects not fully controlled

### 6.3 Educational Attainment by Generation

**Finding (Eurostat 2024 — Tier 1):**
- Young non-EU citizens are **3× more likely** to drop out of education early: **25.3% vs. 8.2%** for nationals
- Early school leaving rate for young non-EU men: **28.2%** vs. **9.8%** for national men (2023)
- Tertiary attainment (ages 25–34): nationals **45.1%**, non-EU citizens **36.7%**
- Native-born individuals with one foreign-born parent have the **highest tertiary rates** in some countries (e.g. Cyprus, Spain)
- **Source:** Eurostat Migrant Integration Statistics — Education (2024) — Tier 1
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Migrant_integration_statistics_-_education
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong

**Finding — PISA trends (2006–2022):** The immigrant-native academic achievement gap **increased** over this period in EU countries, even as the same gap narrowed in English-speaking immigration countries (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Singapore). EU educational systems are systematically less effective at equalising outcomes for immigrant-background students than their English-speaking counterparts.
- **Source:** *Learning and Instruction* / ScienceDirect (2025) — six waves of PISA data 2006–2022
- **URL:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191491X25001130
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — six PISA waves; comparative design; peer-reviewed

**Finding — second-generation:** Second-generation students significantly outperform first-generation but still show measurable gap vs. native-born peers in most EU countries. Parental education *relative to host society* (not absolute) is the key predictor — parents educated abroad but underemployed still transmit educational aspirations.
- **Source:** OECD PISA 2022; *Social Science Research* / ScienceDirect (2024)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on PISA pattern; 🟫 Moderate on mechanism

### 6.4 Intermarriage Rates

**Finding:** Intermarriage rates vary substantially by country and origin group. France: ~27% of individuals born in France to immigrant parents married non-migrant partners (early 2010s). Germany: ~16% of Turkish-background individuals married non-migrant partners (BAMF, 2011). UK and France show higher intermarriage rates than Hungary and Bulgaria. Second-generation individuals have significantly higher intermarriage rates than first-generation across all studies.
- **Source:** *Nationalities Papers* / Cambridge Core; Lanzieri (Academia.edu)
- **URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/nationalities-papers/article/dynamics-of-intermarriage-in-europe/841972E4699946B77B1BC80F0BFE0279
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak — no consistent EU-wide time series; no official EU integration indicator includes intermarriage (Lanzieri notes this as a measurement gap)

### 6.5 Residential Segregation

**Finding:** Non-EU migrants are more residentially segregated than EU migrants in almost every EU country studied. Belgium (Brussels, Antwerp, Liège) shows the strongest spatial isolation of ethnically deprived groups; non-EU migrant neighbourhoods correlate with lowest employment, income, and tertiary education rates. Sweden shows increasing clustering in already-concentrated neighbourhoods. Germany is relatively low nationally; Ruhr cities above average. Southern EU (Italy, Spain) shows lower dissimilarity indices than Northern/Western Europe. Socioeconomic inequality plays as large a role as ethnicity in shaping residential patterns — distinct from the US where race alone drives segregation.
- **Sources:** EU JRC D4I Report (JRC115159) — 2011 Census, 8 EU member states; Benassi et al. (2020) *Spatial Demography*; Benassi et al. (2023) *International Migration*
- **URLs:** https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC115159 | https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40980-020-00064-5
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on patterns; 🟫 Moderate on causal mechanisms (socioeconomic vs. ethnic sorting)

### 6.6 Social Trust — Putnam's Research and EU Replications

**Putnam's original finding (USA, 2007):** In the most diverse US communities, neighbours trust one another about **half as much** as in the most homogeneous communities. All civic health indicators are lower in diverse settings. In the short run, diversity reduces social trust and civic participation — even within one's own group ("hunkering down").
- **Source:** Putnam — *"E Pluribus Unum"*, *Scandinavian Political Studies*, 30(2), 137–174 (2007)
- **URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9477.2007.00176.x
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the original US finding; 41 communities, N > 29,000

**EU replication attempts — mixed results:**
- **Cross-national Europe (Gesthuizen et al.):** Putnam's hypothesis "must be refuted" in European societies. Economic inequality and democratic history are more important than diversity.
- **Netherlands (Lanceé & Dronkers, 2008):** Partial replication — ethnic diversity reduces trust in neighbours for both immigrants and natives.
- **Netherlands neighbourhoods (Gijsberts et al.):** Only **frequency of neighbourhood contact** (not broader trust) is significantly reduced by diversity.
- **Norway, Sweden, UK:** No significant relationship between diversity and general trust.
- **PMC (2023) — key resolution:** The diversity-trust relationship is **mediated by segregation at the wider community level**. Diverse neighbourhoods within *integrated wider communities* show no trust penalty. Diverse neighbourhoods within *segregated wider communities* show significant trust decline.
- **Sources:** Multiple; key synthesis at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9887315/
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate on EU replications — context-dependent; segregation is the key moderator, not diversity per se

**Assessment:** The Putnam diversity-trust effect is stronger where segregation, income inequality, and low institutional trust coincide. It is absent or weak in Nordic countries and at the cross-national EU level. Policy implication: addressing segregation matters more than managing diversity per se.

### 6.7 Cultural Value Surveys (WVS / ESS)

**Finding (ESS 2025 — key study):** Value assimilation by immigrants has only a **modest effect** on their integration outcomes across occupational status, social participation, and political participation. Conservation values are the exception — holding more conservative values hinders political participation. Civic integration programmes that assume value adoption will improve integration outcomes are **not well-supported** by this evidence.
- **Source:** Suárez & Hannikainen — *"Integration Gaps Persist Despite Immigrants' Value Assimilation"*, ESS rounds 1–10 (2002–2020), 28 EU countries, N = 261,830; *Frontiers in Sociology* / PMC (2025)
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11997842/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — large N; multiple ESS rounds; peer-reviewed

**Finding (ESS attitude trends):** European acceptance of immigrants has shifted slightly downward since 2020: acceptance of immigrants from poorer non-European countries fell from **62.5% to 59.0%** (2020–2023). Young people under 25 are consistently more accepting; recent decline is driven by over-25s.
- **Source:** ESS Round 11 analysis (RFBerlin, 2023); ESS Round 11 data now includes Estonia and Ukraine
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — survey data; attitude-behaviour gap acknowledged

### 6.8 Religious Practice and Secularisation

**Finding:** First-generation migrants are markedly more religious than native Europeans across all religious affiliations. Second-generation Muslims retain higher religiosity than native peers but are more secularised than their parents. Religious practices **increase in the first years after arrival** (dislocation, community-seeking), then gradually decline with longer residence. Employment and liberal attitudes on gender and sexuality are associated with faster secularisation — a pattern **similar for Muslim and Christian newcomers**.
- **Source:** PMC / *European Journal of Population* (2023) — pooled ESS data, Western Europe 2002–2018; Springer *KZfSS* (2023) — Netherlands study
- **URLs:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036697/ | https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0037768620948478
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — multiple country studies; pooled ESS data; consistent pattern

**Finding — Islamic identity in second generation:** Second-generation Muslims increasingly reconceptualise Islam in **more private, spiritual, and symbolic terms** — less about outward practice, more about personal identity. Norwegian Muslim youth show religious revival — religion replaces ethnicity as primary identity marker. Second-generation Protestants show evidence of religious revival too, suggesting this may reflect minority-identity dynamics, not Islam specifically.
- **Sources:** *Sociology of Religion* (Friberg & Sterri 2021; Kasselstrand & Mahmoudi 2020); ScienceDirect (2022)
- **URLs:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0197918320986767 | https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0147176722001171
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — qualitative and survey data; Scandinavian context may not generalise

### 6.9 Integration Outcomes by Origin Group

The most comprehensive comparative study (ESS rounds 7 & 8, Western Europe):

| Origin group | Structural integration | Cultural value convergence | Generational trajectory |
|---|---|---|---|
| West European / North American | High | Most liberal (at majority level) | No gap |
| Eastern / SE European | Moderate | Less liberal on some indicators | Fast convergence |
| Middle Eastern / N African / Central Asian | Low (all dimensions) | Least liberal | Convergence by 3rd generation |
| Sub-Saharan African | Low (all dimensions) | Less liberal | Convergence by 3rd generation |
| South / Southeast Asian | Moderate-low | Less liberal | Moderate convergence |

**Key finding:** "All groups converge with the majority in the second or third generation, leaving no third-generation groups which are significantly less liberal than their autochthonous peers." However, convergence takes 2–3 generations for MENA and Sub-Saharan African groups — not 1.
- **Source:** PMC / *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* (2021) — ESS rounds 7 & 8, pooled, Western Europe
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8116888/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — ESS data; multi-country; peer-reviewed

**MENA educational heterogeneity:** Within the MENA category, educational composition varies enormously — Syrian, Lebanese, and Palestinian migrants tend to arrive highly qualified; Moroccan, Algerian, Tunisian, and Turkish migrants are disproportionately less educated. Treating MENA as a monolith produces misleading averages.

**Eastern European specificity:** EU freedom of movement enables high return and circular migration rates, reducing long-term integration pressure. Lower cultural distance from West European norms on gender and sexuality produces faster value convergence.

**France specificity:** Intergenerational mobility is particularly low for people of North African, Sub-Saharan African, and Turkish descent — these groups remain the most disadvantaged in the French labour market even in the second generation (CEPR VoxEU).

### 6.10 Islamic Immigration: Cultural and Traditional Effects

This sub-section covers empirically documented effects of Islamic immigration specifically on cultural norms and practices of EU receiving societies. Political commentary is excluded; only survey data and academic research is cited. Sources are identified with researcher institutional orientation where relevant.

---

#### 6.10a Religious Practice: Diverging Trajectories

**Pew Research Center (2017): "Europe's Growing Muslim Population"** — Tier 1
- Methodology: 2,500+ data sources; all EU member states; censuses, Eurostat asylum data, demographic surveys.
- As of 2016: ~25.8 million Muslims in Europe (4.9% of population). Under medium-migration scenario: **57.9 million (11.2%)** by 2050. Muslim fertility in Europe: **2.6 children/woman** vs. 1.6 for non-Muslims.
- ~10% of those raised Muslim in France have switched away (the only country with large enough sample to measure this). Across the study period, ~160,000 more people switched *away* from Islam than converted into it.
- **Note:** The report does not quantify religious devotion, only population size and composition.
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — Tier 1; benchmark methodology for demographic projections

**Martinovic & Verkuyten (2023): ESS longitudinal analysis (2002–2018)** — Tier 2
- Journal: *European Journal of Population* (Springer); open access on PMC.
- Methodology: ESS rounds 1–9; 15 Western European countries; 16-year longitudinal panel.
- **Key finding:** Native Europeans secularize; Muslim immigrant populations maintain stable or *increasing* religiosity. Second-generation Muslims show **rising** religiosity over the study period — contra simple assimilation predictions. Growing *divergence* between native secular trajectory and immigrant Muslim trajectory documented.
- Second-generation Protestants (Sub-Saharan African) also show religious revival, suggesting a minority-identity mechanism, not Islam specifically.
- **Source:** [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10036697/)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — 16-year ESS panel, 15 countries

**Maliepaard et al. (2024): "Secular Socialization vs. Religious Reactivity"** — Tier 2
- Journal: *Ethnic and Racial Studies* (Taylor & Francis).
- Methodology: Longitudinal survey data from England, Germany, Netherlands, Sweden; linear growth models on three religiosity dimensions.
- **Key finding:** Partial support for secular socialization (exposure to non-Muslim peers reduces religiosity marginally). No sustained support for *reactive religiosity* (discrimination does not durably increase religiosity). **High residential segregation** is identified as the primary mechanism sustaining religiosity — limited exposure to secular norms, not religious intensity per se.
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — longitudinal design, but shorter follow-up period

**Summary:** ESS data across multiple studies shows *divergence* — not convergence — in the short run. Native Europeans secularize; Muslim immigrants maintain elevated observance. Second-generation identity salience is stronger than behavioral observance: feeling Muslim persists more robustly than mosque attendance.

#### 6.10b Cultural Values: Gender Equality, Sexual Liberalism, Secular Authority

**WVS placement (Inglehart & Norris, *Sacred and Secular*, Cambridge UP 2004/2011):**
The WVS cross-national cultural map shows Islamic Middle Eastern societies clustered at the extreme traditional/survival values pole; Protestant Northern Europe at the extreme secular-rational/self-expression pole. The sharpest gap between Western and Muslim-majority societies is on *gender equality and sexual liberalisation* — not on democratic values ("eros/demos" distinction). Muslims in WVS are not significantly less democratic but differ sharply on gender and sexuality norms.
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on population-level WVS mapping; 🟫 Moderate for within-EU application (EU Muslims are not representative of Muslim-majority country populations)

**Lisciotto & Steinmetz (2021): "A Threat to the Occident?"** — Tier 2
- Journal: *Frontiers in Political Science* (open access, PMC-indexed).
- Methodology: ESS data; N = 50,000+; Belgium, Germany, France, Sweden.
- Muslim immigrants score significantly **higher on conservation values** (security, tradition, conformity) and **lower on openness to change** than both Christians and the non-religious. Gap is largest in **Sweden** and smallest in Belgium and Germany — host-country context moderates value expression.
- Unexpected counter-finding: Muslims score *higher on universalism* (equal rights, care for the weak) than comparison groups in some countries.
- **Source:** [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8022660/)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — large N; peer-reviewed; cross-national

**Abdelwahab et al. (2025): Migrant World Values Survey — Sweden** — Tier 2
- Journal: *International Journal for Equity in Health* (Springer, open access).
- Methodology: Face-to-face interviews; Migrant World Values Survey (MWVS) 2018–2019; MENA and Sub-Saharan African migrants in Sweden.
- Muslim respondents: adjusted odds ratio (aOR) = **0.44** (95% CI: 0.32–0.63) for accepting divorce, abortion, and homosexuality vs. non-Muslims.
- Living in Sweden 4+ years: aOR = **1.76** for greater acceptance — partial acculturation over time, persistent gap.
- **Source:** [IJEH, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12939-025-02400-x)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — MWVS face-to-face design reduces social desirability bias; Sweden-specific

**Röder & Spierings (2022): Attitudes Toward Homosexuality among European Muslims** — Tier 2
- Journal: *International Migration Review* (Sage).
- Mosque attendance correlates **negatively** with homosexuality acceptance among European Muslims. Religious identity sustains the gap. Perceived anti-Muslim discrimination can strengthen traditionalist attitudes in some countries (inconsistent across studies).
- **Source:** [IMR](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/01979183211041288)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — survey data; causality direction contested

**Ruud Koopmans (2015): "Religious Fundamentalism and Hostility Against Out-groups"** — Tier 2 (contested)
- Journal: *Journal of Ethnic and Migration Studies*, 41(1), pp. 33–57.
- Methodology: Original survey; N ≈ 9,000; Turkish- and Moroccan-origin Muslims + native Christians; Germany, France, Netherlands, Belgium, Austria, Sweden.
- **Key quantitative findings:**
  - **75%** believe there is only one possible interpretation of the Quran, binding for all Muslims
  - **65%** say religious rules are more important than the laws of the country in which they live
  - **44%** hold all three fundamentalism indicators simultaneously (Cronbach's α = .76)
  - **~60%** would not accept a homosexual friend
  - **45%** think Jews cannot be trusted
  - Turkish-origin: 45% consistent fundamentalism; Moroccan-origin: 50%; **Alevi Turks**: comparable to Christians (low)
  - Cross-country finding: Germany and France (less multicultural) show *lower* Muslim fundamentalism than Belgium — undercutting the "reactive religiosity" hypothesis
- **Koopmans explicitly acknowledges:** The absolute number of Christian fundamentalists in Europe is at least as high as Muslim fundamentalists given population size. Fundamentalism "should not be equated with willingness to support or engage in religiously motivated violence."
- **Key critiques:**
  1. The three-item scale conflates orthodox belief with political fundamentalism; agreement that "religious rules > secular law" may reflect personal piety, not theocratic advocacy
  2. The study over-samples countries with specific Turkish/Moroccan migration histories; results may not generalise to all European Muslim populations
  3. Discrimination → marginalisation → reactive conservatism is an alternative causal pathway Koopmans tests but critics dispute his test design
  4. Framing "Muslim immigrants vs. Christian natives" is misleading — most Muslims in Western Europe were born there
- **Source:** [JEMS 2015](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1369183X.2014.935307); [WZB PDF](https://www.wzb.eu/system/files/docs/sv/iuk/ruud_koopmans_religious_fundamentalism_and_out-group_hostility_among_muslims_and_christian.pdf)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — peer-reviewed, widely cited; methodology disputed; findings partially but not fully replicated across independent surveys

**FRA EU-MIDIS II (2017 — Tier 1):** High rates of discrimination against Muslims in employment and public life confirmed. Structural barriers to integration are documented alongside attitude data.
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on discrimination data

#### 6.10c Gender Norms and Women's Outcomes

**Female Labour Force Participation (FLFP):**
Muslim-majority-origin women show systematically lower FLFP than both native EU women and other migrant groups. Gap partially persists into the second generation but narrows substantially. Country-level variation is large.

- **Kanas & Müller (2021)** (*International Migration Review*): ESS multilevel regression finds Muslim religious affiliation has a **statistically significant negative effect** on immigrant women's labour market outcomes, even after controlling for individual religiosity, gender-role attitudes, and demographics. This is the only religious affiliation to show a persisting negative effect after full controls — suggesting something specific to Muslim community norms, not religiosity per se.
  - **Source:** [IMR, 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/01979183211008867)

- **Bosmans et al. (2015)** (*Social Science Research*): ~40% of the Muslim-non-Muslim employment gap is explained by measured individual characteristics (education, language, migration timing). ~60% remains unexplained — attributed to discrimination, unmeasured cultural norms, and network effects.

- **Denomination effect:** Alevi Muslim women have FLFP rates **19–20 pp higher** than Sunni women in Turkey and EU host countries. This intra-Muslim variation illustrates that "Islamic immigration" is not monolithic; denomination is a powerful moderating variable.

- **Source (FLFP):** OECD *"Settling In 2023"*; Eurostat LFS (Tier 1)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on FLFP gap (Tier 1 data); 🟫 Moderate on causal split between discrimination and cultural norms

**Honour-related violence and forced marriage:**
Consistent quantitative data is not available at EU level. Country-level data exists in the Netherlands (police records), Germany (BKA), and UK (Home Office). Papatya (Turkish women's organization) documented 40 honour killings in Germany since 1996. Men from Turkey, Yugoslavia, and Albania are overrepresented 3–5× for partner homicides in Baden-Württemberg studies. Korteweg & Yurdakul (2014) find Netherlands used gender equality framing most inclusively; Germany used it most stigmatisingly.
- **Source:** Korteweg & Yurdakul (2014), *Women's Studies International Forum* — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0277539513001325)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak quantitatively — statistics unreliable due to underreporting and misclassification; comparative policy analysis is Moderate

**Female Genital Mutilation (FGM):**
EIGE (2013/2015) estimates: ~180,000 girls at risk (UK), ~65,000 (France), ~45,000 (Germany) based on 2012 data. These are extrapolated "at risk" estimates, not confirmed cases. FGM is primarily a regional/cultural practice, not exclusively Islamic — it is practised across religious communities in Sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Counter-finding:** Berg & Denison (2013, Scandinavian study): majority trend toward *rejection* of FGM among longer-settled migrants. Sweden, Germany, and Netherlands studies confirm declining support across generations.
- **Source:** EIGE — [Estimation of Girls at Risk of FGM in the EU (2015)](https://eige.europa.eu/sites/default/files/documents/MH0215093ENN_Web.pdf)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak quantitatively — estimates, not registry data; continuation data is 🟫 Moderate

#### 6.10d Antisemitism

**Jikeli (2015) — ISGAP meta-analysis:**
France: 46% of Muslims with Moroccan nationality agreed with ≥4 of 6 antisemitic statements (vs. 44% with French nationality; 35% other Muslim nationalities). Muslims, far-right sympathisers, and radical-left sympathisers all show elevated antisemitism vs. the general French population. Significant cross-national and intra-Muslim variation.
- **Note on ISGAP:** Normatively oriented toward documenting antisemitism; cross-referencing with broader surveys recommended.
- **Source:** [ISGAP/Jikeli, 2015](https://isgap.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/Jikeli_Antisemitic_Attitudes_among_Muslims_in_Europe1.pdf)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — small Muslim samples in nationally representative surveys; cross-national variation large

**FRA Survey (2024): "Jewish People's Experiences and Perceptions of Antisemitism"** — Tier 1
- N = ~8,000 Jews aged 16+; 13 EU countries; conducted Jan–Jun 2023.
- Four in five Jewish respondents said antisemitism had increased over the past five years. Up to **400% rise** in antisemitic incidents following October 7, 2023.
- **Note:** FRA survey documents Jewish experiences; does not identify perpetrators by religion/ethnicity.
- **Source:** [FRA, 2024](https://fra.europa.eu/sites/default/files/fra_uploads/fra-2024-experiences-perceptions-antisemitism-survey_en.pdf)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on experienced antisemitism; 🟧 Weak on perpetrator attribution

**Social Forces (2025): Cross-national multilevel analysis** — Tier 2
- Methodology: 12 EU countries; tests country-level predictors of antisemitic victimization.
- **Muslim population share** and **unfavourable opinion of Israel** are the strongest country-level predictors of antisemitic victimization — stronger than far-right voting, economic decline, or classical antisemitic attitudes.
- Authors frame this as supporting the "new antisemitism" hypothesis.
- **Methodological caveat:** Ecological analysis; cannot establish individual-level causation; Muslim communities are not homogeneous in political views.
- **Source:** [Social Forces, 2025](https://academic.oup.com/sf/article/103/3/1186/7701118)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — cross-national design is valuable; ecological fallacy risk

**Key methodological warning:** Nationally representative surveys rarely contain enough Muslim respondents for reliable disaggregated analysis. The ecological fallacy (attributing aggregate-level patterns to individuals) is a real risk. Internal variation within Muslim communities by nationality, religiosity, generation, and political orientation is substantial.

#### 6.10e Freedom of Expression

**Key documented incidents:**
- **Rushdie Affair (1989):** *The Satanic Verses* and Khomeini fatwa demonstrated for the first time that European Muslim communities could be mobilised transnationally in response to perceived religious offence. Scholarly framing: precursor event; established Islamic identity as a transnational political force in Europe.
- **Jyllands-Posten cartoons (2005):** Published 12 Muhammad cartoons; diplomatic sanctions from Libya and Saudi Arabia; embassy burnings in Syria, Lebanon, and Indonesia; multiple documented terrorist plots against cartoonist Kurt Westergaard.
- **Charlie Hebdo attack (2015):** 12 killed. Preceded by Islamic organisations' legal challenges (unsuccessful under French law in 2006) and closure of French diplomatic missions in ~20 Muslim countries following 2012 nude Muhammad cartoons.

**"Assassin's Veto" (Timothy Garton Ash, academic concept):** Threats and violence by radical groups produce *de facto* self-censorship in media and publishing, functioning as a veto over expression without requiring formal legal restrictions. Documented through publisher/media behaviour changes following the Rushdie and Jyllands-Posten controversies.

**Survey data (Pew 2017):** Support for Sharia as official law in Western European Muslim communities: **8–12%** in Germany and France (lower than 2006 data of 30–35%). Support for "Islamic courts for family/property disputes" was higher. Survey wording effects are significant; Pew samples may not capture the most isolated subgroups.
- **Source:** Pew Research Center, *"European Muslims: Views on Sharia"* (2017) — Tier 3/4; Ahdar (2019), *MDPI Social Sciences* — [academic analysis of Charlie Hebdo](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0760/8/10/276)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak — survey wording effects; limited large-scale replication since 2017

#### 6.10f Parallel Legal and Social Structures

**UK Home Office Independent Review of Sharia Law (2018)** — Tier 1/2
- Led by Professor Mona Siddiqui (St Andrews University); site visits to four major Sharia councils (Leyton, Birmingham, Nuneaton, Manchester).
- **Key findings:**
  - Estimated **30–85 Sharia councils** operating in England and Wales (lack of systematic data acknowledged)
  - **Over 90% of users are women** seeking Islamic divorces (*talaq*) unavailable through unilateral civil divorce
  - Councils have **no legal authority** under English law; decisions are not enforceable
  - Some councils discriminate on the basis of sex contrary to English law; some make custody decisions based on religious criteria rather than child welfare
  - Women sometimes refused Islamic divorces even when men have been violent
  - Review recommended marriage law reforms to reduce the number of religiously-only-married Muslim women lacking civil law protections
- **Context:** Jewish Beth Din courts and Catholic annulment tribunals operate analogously — as faith-based dispute resolution forums with no authority to override English law
- **Muslim Arbitration Tribunal (MAT):** A legally distinct entity operating under the Arbitration Act 1996; can issue legally binding decisions on commercial disputes if both parties agree and results comply with English law. Criminal and divorce matters are entirely outside its jurisdiction.
- **Assessment:** Evidence does not support the claim of a formal parallel legal system. *De facto* social authority of Sharia councils in some isolated communities — especially where English is not widely spoken and legal literacy is low — constitutes a real if informal pressure on women, but this is legally unenforceable.
- **Source:** [UK Home Office, 2018](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/678478/6.4152_HO_CPFG_Report_into_Sharia_Law_in_the_UK_WEB.pdf)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — official commissioned review; England/Wales only; EU-wide data absent

#### 6.10g Integration Outcomes by Muslim Origin Group (EURISLAM Project)

The EURISLAM project (2010–2015; UNIGE; six European countries) surveyed four Muslim-origin groups (Turks, Moroccans, ex-Yugoslavians, Pakistanis) plus majority samples across Belgium, France, Germany, Netherlands, Switzerland, UK.

| Origin group | Religious conservatism | FLFP | Labour market | Key specificity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turkish-origin (Sunni) | High (45% fundamentalism scale) | Low–moderate | Below average | DIYANET mosque infrastructure sustains religious boundary-maintenance |
| Turkish-origin (Alevi) | Comparable to native Christians | High | Moderate | Secularized during Ottoman/Republican transition; lowest conservatism of all Muslim groups |
| Moroccan-origin | Highest (50% fundamentalism scale) | Low | Below average | High variability in France; higher French intermarriage rates than Turkish-origin |

#### 6.10h Second-Generation Convergence and Divergence Debate

**For convergence (large-N):**
- Suárez & Hannikainen (2025, ESS N = 261,830): All migrant groups, including Muslim-origin, converge toward host-country secular-rational values across generations. Third-generation show no significant difference from autochthonous peers. Convergence takes 2–3 generations for MENA. **This remains the most important counter-finding to cultural incompatibility claims.**
- Maliepaard & Phalet (2019): Among second-generation Dutch Muslims, religiosity is *unrelated* to educational attainment, labour market participation, or national identification. Religion has decoupled from structural integration in the Dutch second generation.
- Fleischmann & Phalet (2024): Levels of religiosity and religious cognitions among Turkish Muslims in Germany are largely unrelated to educational attainment, labour market participation, intergroup contact, or national belonging. Discrimination and network effects explain more variance.

**For limited/slow convergence:**
- Koopmans (2015): Second-generation Muslims show fundamentalism rates comparable to the first generation; simple generational secularisation model is not confirmed.
- Drouhot & Nee (working paper): In France, Muslims are substantially more religious than socially comparable natives regardless of generation.
- Martinovic & Verkuyten (2023): Growing divergence in religious trajectories in the short run — native secularisation vs. Muslim stable/increasing religiosity.

**Methodological challenge — Statham (2024): "Challenging the Muslimification of Muslims"**
- Journal: *Journal of Ethnic and Migration Studies*, 50(1).
- **Ethnonational family origin** (Turkish, Moroccan, Pakistani, etc.) explains more variance in "liberal democratic values" than religious self-identification as Muslim.
- **Religiosity** has modest impact on value divergence; self-identification with the settlement country is a stronger and opposite predictor.
- Concept: **"Muslimification of Muslims"** — the tendency of research to reduce Muslim complexity to religious identity, mirroring political/media discourse; identifies this as "a sociological imagination deficit and methodological flaw."
- **Source:** [JEMS, 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369183X.2023.2268894)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — large survey data; important methodological contribution; contested by Koopmans school

## Counter-Evidence & Debates

**Against Putnam:** Cross-national EU evidence does not confirm the diversity-trust relationship. Nordic countries with high diversity and low segregation show no trust decline. The dominant counter-explanation is that segregation (not diversity) drives trust erosion — and that Putnam's US findings reflect specifically American patterns of racial segregation.

**Against cultural incompatibility claims:** Third-generation value convergence is documented across all origin groups, including MENA (ESS 2025 study, N = 261,830). The question is not whether convergence happens but over how many generations. Critics of the convergence thesis note that 2–3 generation timelines involve 60–90 years of cultural transition, which is non-trivial in terms of social policy.

**Against integration pessimism:** The Belgian matched-employer study shows the ethnic wage penalty disappears in the second generation when employer effects are controlled. This suggests **employer discrimination**, not human capital deficiency, drives the first-generation wage gap.

**Against Koopmans:** His 2015 Islamic fundamentalism findings used small per-country samples and data from a single ESS round. The ESS 2025 large-N study (N = 261,830) found value assimilation is occurring, even if slowly. Maliepaard & Phalet (2019, Netherlands) and Fleischmann & Phalet (2024, Germany) find that second-generation Muslim religiosity is *unrelated* to structural integration outcomes — undercutting the claim that religious conservatism causes integration failure. Statham (2024) finds that ethnonational origin explains more variance than religious identity, challenging the Muslimification frame. Critics argue Koopmans conflates conservative religious views with anti-democratic or violent tendencies; Koopmans himself explicitly states fundamentalism "should not be equated with willingness to engage in religious violence."

## Confounding Factors

- **Socioeconomic status:** Most measured integration gaps correlate with poverty and low education as much as with migration background. Disentangling ethnicity from class is methodologically difficult.
- **Discrimination:** Belgian employer-employee data shows discrimination accounts for part of the wage gap independent of human capital. Studies that do not control for discrimination understate integration success.
- **Geographic concentration:** Studies of segregated urban neighbourhoods will show worse integration outcomes than national-level analyses. Both levels are valid but should not be conflated.
- **Refugee vs. labour migrant:** Refugees show worse integration outcomes on virtually all dimensions compared to labour migrants of similar origin. Studies that pool these groups overestimate average integration failure.
- **Gender:** Sons of immigrants consistently show worse mobility than daughters in multiple European studies. Male-specific barriers (labour market discrimination, peer effects, identity conflicts) require gender-disaggregated analysis.
- **Religion measurement:** Survey questions about religious belief, practice, and identity capture different dimensions. High practice rates do not necessarily predict cultural or civic incompatibility.

---

## Evidence Gaps

1. **EU-wide intermarriage statistics** — No official EU integration indicator includes intermarriage. Data is country-specific and methodologically inconsistent.
2. **Third-generation data** — Most studies cover first and second generation. True third-generation samples are small in most EU countries. Projected convergence relies on extrapolation.
3. **Honour violence and forced marriage statistics** — Systematically absent at EU level; inconsistent at member-state level.
4. **Long-run religious trajectory** — Whether second-generation Muslim religious revival (Martinovic & Verkuyten 2023) is a transitional phase or a stable equilibrium is not established. Current data covers at most 30–40 years of post-immigration observation.
5. **Causal mechanisms for integration failure** — Discrimination, residential segregation, school system quality, and cultural distance all contribute to integration gaps, but their relative contributions are not well-identified causally. The causal direction between conservatism and integration outcomes is genuinely contested.
6. **EU-wide data on Sharia councils and parallel social structures** — UK Home Office 2018 review covers England/Wales only. No systematic EU-wide mapping of equivalent faith-based dispute-resolution bodies exists.
7. **Within-Muslim variation underresearched** — Most survey instruments do not disaggregate by denomination (Sunni vs. Alevi vs. Ahmadiyya), country of origin, or generation within the "Muslim" category, producing misleading averages.

## Bottom Line

Integration is generationally progressive but slow for the largest non-EU origin groups. First-generation non-EU migrants (especially MENA and Sub-Saharan African) show significant gaps in employment, education, income, civic participation, and cultural values relative to native-born populations. Second-generation outcomes are substantially better across all dimensions. Third-generation convergence in cultural values — including attitudes toward gender equality and sexual orientation — is empirically documented even for groups with the largest first-generation gaps, but this takes 60–90 years (2–3 generations) for MENA populations.

Social trust is not straightforwardly reduced by diversity. Segregation, not diversity, is the key moderating variable. Putnam's US findings do not reliably replicate in European contexts.

Value assimilation occurs but is a weak predictor of integration outcomes. Labour market access, language training with credential recognition, and anti-discrimination enforcement have stronger empirical support as integration levers.

Islamic immigration introduces the largest average cultural value distance of any major migrant group (WVS mapping). This is documented in multiple independent surveys (WVS, ESS, MWVS). The convergence evidence (ESS N = 261,830) shows this distance narrows but does not disappear until the third generation for MENA populations. However, origin group, denomination, and host-country context are stronger predictors of integration trajectory than "Muslim" identity per se: ex-Yugoslav and Alevi Turkish migrants integrate on dramatically different trajectories from Pakistani or Moroccan-origin migrants. Research framing that aggregates all Muslim-origin populations produces systematically distorted averages.

The policy-relevant question is how to manage a 60–90 year value-convergence transition while maintaining democratic norms and equal rights protections for all residents — including Muslim women affected by community-internal gender norms. That is a normative question beyond the scope of this empirical review.

**Confidence level:** High on employment/education gap data (Tier 1 Eurostat). High on generational improvement pattern (multiple independent studies). Moderate on value convergence trajectory (ESS multi-wave, good N, but extrapolation to 3rd generation is inferential). Moderate on religious practice divergence (consistent ESS pattern across multiple studies). Moderate on Putnam EU replications (context-dependent). Moderate on Koopmans fundamentalism findings (peer-reviewed, widely cited, but methodology disputed). Low on honour violence, FGM, and informal religious practice data (measurement gaps severe). Low on EU-wide parallel social structure data (only UK Home Office data exists).
