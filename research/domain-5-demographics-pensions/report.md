# Domain 5: Demographics & Pension Sustainability

**Research date:** 2026-02-22
**Evidence covers:** Fertility rates, dependency ratios, pension projections, population scenarios
**Primary sources:** Eurostat EUROPOP2023, EC 2024 Ageing Report, OECD Pensions at a Glance 2025, UN DESA WPP 2024, IMF WP 2025, Bruegel WP 2025

---

## Evidence Summary

The best available evidence — from Eurostat, the European Commission, OECD, UN DESA, and IMF — converges on the same structural conclusion: migration is a *necessary but insufficient* instrument for addressing EU demographic decline. It moderates the worsening of dependency ratios in the short to medium term but cannot stabilise them without volumes that would be politically and practically infeasible. The EU TFR hit a historic low of 1.38 in 2023. Migrant fertility is higher on arrival but converges rapidly toward host-country norms — typically halving the differential within five years, with near-complete second-generation convergence for most origin groups. Pension spending pressure is real but manageable at the EU average level; individual countries (Spain, Greece) face severe structural stress regardless of migration policy.

---

## Key Findings

### 5.1 EU Fertility Baseline

**Finding:** The EU total fertility rate (TFR) reached a **historic low of 1.38 in 2023**, down from 1.46 in 2022 — a 5.4% drop in total births (3.67 million), the largest annual decline since 1961. Country range: Malta 1.06 and Spain 1.12 at the bottom; France 1.66 and Hungary 1.55 at the top.
- **Source:** Eurostat Fertility Statistics (updated 2025) — Tier 1
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Fertility_statistics
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — Tier 1 statistical data; administrative births registration

---

### 5.2 Migrant Fertility: Initial Rates and Convergence

**Finding:** Migrants — particularly from Sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia — arrive with TFRs above the host-country average. The initial fertility gap **halves within five years of arrival**. Migrants who arrived as children show differentials that are small and largely statistically insignificant. Origin group matters significantly: South Asian and Turkish-origin women maintain elevated third-birth rates for longer; Sub-Saharan African-origin women converge faster; Southeast Asian descendants adopt host-country norms almost entirely.
- **Source:** Kulu et al. (2019), *European Journal of Population* / PMC; *Demographic Research* Vol. 52, Art. 33 (2025)
- **Methodology:** Multistate event-history modelling; retrospective birth histories; EU-LFS data
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7363754/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — replicated across multiple countries and time periods

**Finding (second generation):** Second-generation immigrants largely adopt the host-country two-child norm. In France, descendants from Southeast Asia and North Africa show virtually identical fertility patterns to native French women. Turkish-origin descendants remain an outlier with earlier and slightly higher fertility but still well below replacement pressure levels.
- **Source:** Springer Nature (2024); ScienceDirect Norway register study (2023)
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12134-024-01121-4
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — register data, longitudinal; multi-country replication

**OECD assessment:** "The net contribution of migrants to overall fertility levels is relatively small." Even with migration, the gain to the overall TFR is marginal — because (a) migrant fertility converges toward native levels and (b) migrants age into the old-age population themselves over time.
- **Source:** OECD Society at a Glance 2024
- **URL:** https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/society-at-a-glance-2024_918d8db3-en/

---

### 5.3 Dependency Ratios

**Finding:** The EU old-age dependency ratio (persons 65+ per 100 persons aged 20–64) stood at **34.5 in 2025** (approximately 3 workers per retiree). Eurostat projects this rises to **55 by 2050** and **65 by 2100** under baseline assumptions.
- **Source:** Eurostat Population Structure and Ageing Statistics (2025) — Tier 1
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Population_structure_and_ageing
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on current figure; ⬛ Strong on direction of trend; individual-year projections carry growing uncertainty

**Finding:** Labour migrants improve the dependency ratio — but only durably if they are temporary. The longer migrants remain, the more their own ageing shifts them into the dependent population, diminishing the net demographic return. Per De Beer (SAGE, 2024): "Immigration can only prevent population ageing at unprecedented, unsustainable and increasing levels of inflow."
- **Source:** OECD Society at a Glance 2024; Paul de Beer, *SAGE Journals* (2024); PNAS (2021)
- **Methodology:** Demographic projection modelling
- **URLs:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17816858241240595 | https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1918988117
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — consistent across multiple independent modelling exercises

### 5.4 Can Migration Realistically Offset EU Demographic Decline?

**The landmark quantitative answer — UN DESA "Replacement Migration" (2001):**

The UN ran four scenarios for the EU covering 1995–2050. Results remain the authoritative quantitative baseline:

| Scenario | Objective | Annual migrants required |
|---|---|---|
| I — Natural increase only | — | 0 |
| II — Maintain 1995 population | Maintain total population | ~949,000/year |
| III — Maintain working-age population | Maintain labour force size | Larger than Scenario II |
| IV — Maintain 1995 support ratio (4.8) | Maintain pension system ratios | ~6.1 million/year (total 674 million, 2015–2040) |

- Scenarios II–III: achievable relative to historical flows
- **Scenario IV: explicitly deemed "impossible"** — would require 674 million immigrants in 25 years
- **Source:** UN DESA *"Replacement Migration"* (2001)
- **URL:** https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/sites/www.un.org.development.desa.pd/files/unpd-egm_200010_un_2001_replacementmigration.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong as a modelling framework; the specific figures are now dated (2025 flows substantially exceed 2001 projections)

**Eurostat EUROPOP2023 zero-migration scenario:**
- Baseline: EU population peaks at **453.3 million in 2026**, falls to **419.5 million by 2100** (−6.1% from 2022)
- Zero migration: EU population falls approximately **34% by 2100** relative to 2022
- Most severely affected under zero migration: Italy (−52%), Spain (−49%), Malta (−48%), Portugal/Greece (−44%)
- In baseline, net migration of 2.6 per thousand/year only *partially compensates* for natural decrease of 3 per thousand/year — the EU is already in natural population decline
- **Source:** Eurostat EUROPOP2023
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Population_projections_in_the_EU
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong as scenario modelling — not forecasts

**EC Joint Research Centre (2025):** If labour participation rates remain at 2011–2022 averages, EU labour force shrinks by **20.2% by 2070** — equivalent to **42.8 million fewer workers** or approximately 1 million workers lost per year until 2050.
- **Source:** EC JRC (June 2025)
- **URL:** https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/jrc-news-and-updates/tackling-eus-shrinking-workforce-better-education-more-women-jobs-skilled-migration-2025-06-11_en
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — EC administrative and LFS data

### 5.5 Pension System Projections

**EC 2024 Ageing Report** (the definitive EU institutional reference, covering all 27 EU member states + Norway, 2022–2070):
- EU average public pension spending: **11.4% of GDP (2022) → 12.1% by 2045 → 11.8% by 2070** (+0.4 pp net over 48 years)
- Total age-related spending (pensions + health + long-term care + education): **24.4% of GDP (2022) → +1.2 pp by 2070**
- **Spain** is the extreme outlier: pension spending rises from **13.1% to 16.7% of GDP by 2070**
- **Italy**: pension spending *falls* from 15.6% to 13.7% — due to prior structural reforms
- Sensitivity test finding: **higher migration produces the largest positive effect on growth** among all tested variables, exceeding the effect of higher productivity alone
- **Source:** European Commission 2024 Ageing Report (April 2024)
- **URL:** https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/publications/2024-ageing-report-economic-and-budgetary-projections-eu-member-states-2022-2070_en
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — triennial EC institutional publication using EUROPOP2023 baseline; covers all 27 member states

**OECD Pensions at a Glance 2025:**
- OECD average old-age dependency ratio: **33 per 100 working-age (2025) → 52 per 100 by 2050**
- Public pension spending: **8.8% of GDP (2023) → 10.0% by 2050** (OECD average)
- EU-27 average: **9.9% GDP (2023) → 10.9% by 2050**, then stabilises
- Countries facing dependency ratio increases of >25 pp by 2050: Greece, Italy, Poland, Slovakia, Spain
- Luxembourg case: social security contributor-to-pensioner ratio falls from **2.3 in 2022 to below 1 by 2070**
- **Source:** OECD Pensions at a Glance 2025
- **URL:** https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2025/11/pensions-at-a-glance-2025_76510fe4.html
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — OECD cross-national data synthesis

**Bruegel Working Paper 24/2025** (Pinkus & Kirkegaard): "A low probability of a sustained rebound in fertility rates effectively leaves the EU with continuously high positive net-migration levels as the only route to mitigate the economic effects of ageing." Germany's projected potential growth: **0.3–0.4% annually in 2025–2029** due to negative labour input growth — described as "catastrophically low."
- **Source:** Bruegel WP 24/2025
- **Orientation note:** Bruegel is economic-liberal, technocratic — generally favourable to managed migration as economic instrument
- **URL:** https://www.bruegel.org/working-paper/macroeconomic-impact-ageing-eu-immigration-policy-and-pension-expenditures
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — modelling exercise; scenario-dependent; Bruegel orientation noted

**IMF Working Paper "Lost Generations?" (Cevik, March 2025):** Using instrumental variable estimation across 42 European countries 1960–2022, lower fertility has a **significant negative effect on GDP per capita growth** in the long run. Short run: lower fertility temporarily raises working-age share. Long run: old-age dependency burden dominates. Without large migration inflows or structural reforms, negative consequences are "large."
- **Source:** IMF WP 2025/055 (March 2025)
- **URL:** https://www.imf.org/en/publications/wp/issues/2025/03/21/lost-generations-fertility-and-economic-growth-in-europe-565380
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — IV estimation addresses endogeneity; 42-country scope; but fertility-growth causality mechanisms are contested in the literature

### 5.6 Second-Generation Demographic Patterns

**Labour force participation:** Second-generation immigrants (native-born with at least one immigrant parent) face a persistent employment gap vs. comparable native-born workers, especially men of non-European origin. The gap is present across EU countries regardless of welfare state model. Origin group matters considerably.
- **Source:** CEPR VoxEU (2023); MIPEX (2025)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — cross-country comparisons; limited causal identification

**Education (Eurostat 2024):** Adults aged 25–34: tertiary attainment rate = **45.1%** (nationals), **36.7%** (non-EU citizens), **41.1%** (other EU citizens). Gap is closing (3.7 pp since 2015) but persists.
- **Source:** Eurostat Migrant Integration Statistics (2024) — Tier 1
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong

**Population share:** Across EU-14, first-generation immigrants = 16% of population aged 0–74; second generation = 6%. Second-generation share reaches 10% in Luxembourg, ~8% in Belgium, Germany, Switzerland; ~2% in Greece, Lithuania, Portugal.
- **Source:** *Nature / Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* (2023)
- **URL:** https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-023-02482-4
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — derived estimates; methodological variation in who counts as "second generation" across countries

## Counter-Evidence & Debates

**Against "migration solves ageing":** David Coleman (Oxford, 2006, 2008) provided the most rigorous academic critique of the replacement-migration thesis. His core finding: immigration cannot solve population ageing except through "very high and exponentially increasing inflows." The only sustainable solution requires avoiding "excessively low birth rates." His projections of foreign-origin populations reaching 25–35% of Western European countries by mid-century are widely cited by both restrictionist and mainstream demographers — the methodology is sound even if the political framing varies by who cites it.

**Against "migration is irrelevant to demographics":** The EUROPOP2023 zero-migration scenarios show catastrophic population declines in Southern and Eastern Europe without any migration. This is not contested. The debate is about *how much* migration helps and at *what cost* — not whether migration has demographic effect.

**On fertility convergence:** The convergence thesis (migrant fertility rapidly converges to host norms) is well-supported for most origin groups. The outlier is Pakistani/Bangladeshi-origin populations in the UK and Turkish-origin populations in Continental Europe, which show slower second-generation convergence. This is a *real* exception to the general pattern, not a strawman.

---

## Confounding Factors

- **Selection effects:** Who migrates matters. Labour migrants who self-select for migration tend to be younger, healthier, and more educated than average in their origin country ("healthy migrant effect"). This inflates short-term demographic benefit estimates.
- **Duration:** Whether migrants stay temporarily or permanently is the single most important variable for long-term demographic impact. Circular and temporary migrants provide demographic benefit without the compounding of their own ageing costs. Current EU data on stay duration is incomplete.
- **Return migration:** If high-skilled migrants return home after their working years (plausible for EU intra-migrants), the host country bears costs of settlement and receives labour-market benefits but loses the pension contribution years. Not well measured.
- **Mortality differentials:** Migrant populations in some EU countries show lower mortality rates ("healthy migrant effect"), which prolongs their working-age contribution but also extends their pension-receipt period.
- **Fertility policy confound:** France and Hungary have the highest EU TFRs (1.66 and 1.55 respectively) — both have active pro-natalist policies, in very different political contexts. This suggests policy can influence fertility independently of migration, complicating attribution.

## Disaggregated Analysis

| Dimension | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Origin — Sub-Saharan Africa** | Higher initial TFR; faster convergence than expected |
| **Origin — Middle East / Turkey** | Higher initial TFR; slower second-generation convergence (Turkey); elevated third-birth rates persist |
| **Origin — South/East Asia** | Faster convergence; second-generation near-identical to natives |
| **Origin — Eastern Europe (EU)** | TFR close to home-country norms, which are themselves low (Romania, Poland TFR ~1.3–1.4) |
| **Time period — pre-2015** | Slower migration flows; demographic effects well-studied |
| **Time period — 2015–2016 crisis** | Asylum-heavy inflow; demographic contribution less favourable in short term |
| **Time period — 2022+ (Ukraine)** | High-education, high-female cohort; faster labour market integration; TFR close to EU average |
| **Country — Southern EU (Italy, Spain, Greece)** | Demographic dependence on migration is most acute; without it, population collapses by 2100 in projections |
| **Country — Northern EU (Sweden, Germany, NL)** | Migration moderates but does not prevent ageing; pension reform remains essential regardless |
| **Second generation vs. first** | Near-convergence in fertility for most groups; persistent labour market gap; closing education gap |

## Evidence Gaps

1. **Circular/temporary migration data:** Eurostat tracks stock and annual flow but does not robustly capture duration-of-stay patterns, making it impossible to precisely model the demographic return to temporary vs. permanent inflows.
2. **Post-2022 cohort (Ukraine):** Too recent for long-term modelling. Ukraine cohort demographics are unusual (majority women, higher education); long-term stay probability is uncertain and politics-dependent.
3. **Fertility policy vs. migration interaction:** No rigorous study has isolated the independent effects of pro-natalist policy vs. migration composition changes on national TFR trends.
4. **Second-generation labour outcomes disaggregated by EU country:** The data exists at country level but consistent cross-EU comparisons for second-generation pension contribution modelling are not available.
5. **Endogenous migration response:** Most demographic models treat future migration as an exogenous policy variable. In reality, ageing societies become less attractive to young migrants as public service quality declines — a feedback loop not modelled in current projections.

---

## Bottom Line

The evidence is structurally clear and convergent across all major institutional sources (Eurostat, EC, OECD, IMF, UN):

1. **Migration slows EU demographic decline but cannot stop it.** Maintaining population size and working-age population through migration is feasible at historically observed flow rates. Maintaining 1990s-era pension support ratios through migration alone requires volumes that are politically and practically impossible.

2. **EU pension systems face real but largely manageable average pressure** (+0.4 pp of GDP over 48 years at EU average), masking severe country-level stress (Spain +3.6 pp, Luxembourg support ratio below 1 by 2070).

3. **Migrant fertility is higher on arrival but converges rapidly.** The idea that migrant fertility will permanently resolve low EU birth rates is not supported by the evidence. Convergence is documented across all major origin groups within one to two generations, with Turkish-origin populations as the most persistent exception.

4. **The honest policy conclusion from the demographic literature** (Coleman, UN DESA, OECD, EC) is identical: migration is necessary, helpful, and insufficient on its own. Structural pension reforms (retirement age, contribution rates, indexation rules) are unavoidable regardless of migration policy choices.

**Confidence level:** High on fertility convergence findings (replicated, register data). High on pension projection trajectories (EC/OECD institutional models). Moderate on long-term population scenarios (uncertainty grows rapidly past 2060). Low on second-generation pension contribution modelling (insufficient longitudinal data).
