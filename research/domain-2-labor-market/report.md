# Domain 2: Labor Market Effects

**Research date:** 2026-02-22
**Evidence covers:** Wage effects, employment displacement, complementarity/substitution, women's labour participation, informal economy, occupational segregation
**Primary sources:** CEPII meta-analysis 2025, OECD IMO 2025, IZA, EC, Eurostat, peer-reviewed journals (QJE, RES, JEEA, EJ, AEJ:Applied)

---

## Evidence Summary

The labour market effects of immigration in the EU are heterogeneous, distributional, and heavily mediated by institutional context. The average wage effect on native workers is centered around zero in meta-analytic syntheses, but this average conceals meaningful distributional effects: workers at the bottom of the wage distribution face negative wage pressure while those above the median benefit from complementarities. Short-run employment displacement effects are modest and fade in the long run; effects are concentrated on low-educated native workers and attenuated by employment protection legislation. Immigration strongly increased native women's labour force participation through household production and childcare channels. Occupational segregation is severe: 38.5% of tertiary-educated migrants work below their qualification level. Informal economy participation is structurally higher among irregular migrants but is poorly measured. Of 4.2 million EU jobs created between 2019–2023, approximately 64% were filled by non-EU citizens.

## Key Findings

### 2.1 Wage Effects — Meta-Analytic Synthesis

**Finding:** The average wage effect of immigration on native workers is **centered around zero**, with substantial heterogeneity across methodology, skill group, and institutional context. Shift-share IV strategies correct upward OLS bias (immigrants self-select into booming labour markets, causing OLS to overstate positive effects). Low-skilled immigration creates substitution effects with similarly-skilled native workers; high-skilled immigration generates complementarities. Earlier consensus (Longhi et al. 2005/2010): a 1 percentage point increase in the immigrant labour share lowers native wages by approximately **0.6%** — economically modest.
- **Source:** Nedoncelle, Marchal, Aubry & Héricourt — *"Does Immigration Affect Native Wages? A Meta-Analysis"*, CEPII Working Paper 2025-07. Meta-analysis of **88 studies, 2,992 reduced-form estimates**, published 1985–2023.
- **URL:** https://www.cepii.fr/PDF_PUB/wp/2025/wp2025-07.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — largest available meta-analysis; covers three methodological traditions; corrects for publication bias

**Finding — wage distribution (UK):** Immigration **depresses wages below the 20th percentile** of the native wage distribution and **slightly raises wages above the median**. Overall native wage effect is slightly positive. Mechanism: immigrants downgrade significantly on arrival, concentrating competition at the very bottom while increasing complementary demand for native cognitive/communication skills above.
- **Source:** Dustmann, Frattini & Preston — *"The Effect of Immigration along the Distribution of Wages"*, *Review of Economic Studies*, 80(1), pp. 145–173 (2013). Updated: Dustmann, Kastis & Preston (2023) confirms patterns persist 1994–2016.
- **URL:** https://www.homepages.ucl.ac.uk/~uctpb21/Cpapers/Review%20of%20Economic%20Studies-2013-Dustmann-145-73.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — peer-reviewed; UK Labour Force Survey; replicated in update

**Finding — gender selection bias (France):** Raw correlations show no correlation between immigration and female wages, but this reflects selection bias: native women most adversely affected by immigration *exit the labour market* and are excluded from wage samples. After correcting for selection, the **wage elasticity for women becomes negative and similar in magnitude to men**. Immigration reduced native women's employment rates substantially while leaving an upward-biased wage signal in the employed sample.
- **Source:** Borjas & Edo — *"Gender, Selection into Employment, and the Wage Impact of Immigration"*, IZA DP No. 14261 / NBER WP 28682; forthcoming *Journal of Labor Economics* (2026)
- **URL:** https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/14261/gender-selection-employment-and-wage-impact-immigration
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — important methodological point; single-country study; not yet peer-published (forthcoming JLE 2026)

### 2.2 The Borjas vs. Card/Peri Debate

This is the foundational methodological debate in migration economics. Summary for context:

| Position | Approach | Key finding | Key weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Borjas (2003)** | National skill-cell; perfect substitutability within cells | Immigration lowered least-educated US wages by **~9%** (1980–2000); elasticity −0.3 to −0.4 | Assumes perfect substitutability; holds capital fixed; US context |
| **Card/Peri** | Spatial/regional variation; shift-share IV | Wage effects **very small or zero** for natives | Spatial approach vulnerable to native mobility response |
| **Ottaviano & Peri (2012)** | Imperfect substitutability within cells | Relaxing perfect-substitution → negligible or positive native wage effects | Borjas identified a data coding error in original study |
| **CEPII 2025 meta-analysis** | All three approaches across 88 studies | Average near zero; mixture approach produces larger negative estimates | — |

**Current status:** No methodological consensus. The CEPII 2025 meta-analysis is the most comprehensive synthesis available. The debate's key lesson: results are sensitive to assumptions about (a) whether immigrants and natives are substitutes within skill groups, (b) capital adjustment, and (c) native mobility in response to immigration.

### 2.3 Employment Displacement Effects

**Finding:** Across European regions 2010–2019, the overall impact of immigration on native employment-to-population ratios is **modest**. Short-run adverse effects exist, particularly for low-educated natives, but **disappear in the long run**. Low-educated native workers experience employment losses in the short run; high-educated natives experience employment gains. Immigration increased the **employment opportunity gap between high- and low-educated natives** over the decade. Regions with stricter employment protection laws (EPL) and collective bargaining coverage show significantly **smaller displacement effects**. Economically dynamic regions absorb immigrants with little or no effect on natives.
- **Source:** Edo & Ozguzel — *"The Impact of Immigration on the Employment Dynamics of European Regions"*, IZA DP No. 16469; OECD Regional Development Papers No. 52 (2023); published in *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* (2023)
- **URLs:** https://docs.iza.org/dp16469.pdf | https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/migration-and-employment-dynamics-across-european-regions
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — multi-country EU panel; shift-share IV; peer-reviewed

**Critical institutional moderator:** D'Amuri & Peri (2014) confirm that job reallocation by native workers in response to immigration is **5–6 times larger in the US than in Europe**, attributed to Europe's stricter EPL limiting occupational mobility. This means complementarity benefits accrue less in rigid EU labour markets than flexible ones.

### 2.4 Complementarity vs. Substitution

**Finding — task specialisation:** Foreign-born workers specialise in manual-physical labour-intensive occupations; native-born workers specialise in communication-language-intensive occupations. Native workers in regions with large immigration inflows **upgrade into communication-intensive, better-paid jobs** in response. This task reallocation **mitigates negative wage consequences** for less-educated native workers. Mechanism: immigrants' language limitations create comparative advantage in manual tasks, increasing demand for native cognitive and communication skills.
- **Source:** Peri & Sparber — *"Task Specialization, Immigration, and Wages"*, *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 1(3), pp. 135–169 (2009)
- **URL:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.1.3.135
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — US study; task-specialisation mechanism confirmed in Europe but weaker due to EPL rigidity

**Finding — European occupational upgrading:** Across 14 EU countries 1996–2007, immigrants occupied manual-routine occupations; native workers responded by moving toward abstract-interactive (cognitive) occupations. A doubling of the immigrant share was associated with a **0.7% increase in native wages** via this upward mobility mechanism. Effect is weakest in countries with rigid EPL.
- **Source:** D'Amuri & Peri — *"Immigration, Jobs, and Employment Protection: Evidence from Europe Before and During the Great Recession"*, *Journal of the European Economic Association*, 12(2), pp. 432–464 (2014)
- **URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jeea.12040
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — 14-country EU panel; peer-reviewed; widely cited

**Finding — imperfect substitutability (UK):** Immigration primarily reduced **wages of other immigrants** — particularly university-educated immigrants — rather than wages of native-born workers. Key mechanism: immigrants downgrade significantly upon arrival, making them compete primarily with prior immigrants rather than equally-qualified natives. This partially explains why regional studies fail to detect wage effects on natives.
- **Source:** Manacorda, Manning & Wadsworth — *"The Impact of Immigration on the Structure of Male Wages"*, *Journal of the European Economic Association* / *Economic Journal* (2012)
- **URLs:** https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1640410 | https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/2352/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — UK micro data; peer-reviewed; consistent with Dustmann (2013)

### 2.5 Effects on Native Women's Labour Market Participation

**Finding — household production channel:** Low-skilled immigrants who provide household and care services enable **high-skilled native women to increase labour force participation** substantially. More than **12 million foreign domestic workers globally** (17.2% of all female migrant workers) serve this function. Evidence shows immigration narrows the gender pay gap in high-powered occupations. Native women invest no less in children and do not have lower birth rates as a result.
- **Source:** NBER Working Paper No. 31234 (2023) — survey/meta-review of global literature; also *European Economic Review* (2023)
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31234/w31234.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — literature survey; effects well-documented but magnitude varies by country

**Finding — childcare channel (Spain):** A 1 percentage point increase in the regional share of immigrants raises the women-men employment probability gap by **0.6 percentage points** in families with children and **0.2 percentage points** for childless families. The **additional 0.4 pp effect in families with children** is specifically attributable to immigrants supplying childcare services.
- **Source:** ECINEQ Working Paper 2022-610 — *"Immigration, Childcare and Gender Differences in the Spanish Labour Market"*
- **URL:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/inq/inqwps/ecineq2022-610.html
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — single-country (Spain); sound methodology

**Finding — care work hierarchy:** Immigrant women are disproportionately concentrated in domestic, care, and cleaning work. Three-quarters of female migrant workers are employed as "domestic, office or hotel cleaners and helpers" or in personal care. This enables native women to access higher-level labour market positions but concentrates migrant women in low-status roles — a distributional trade-off with integration consequences.
- **Source:** *Social Politics: International Studies in Gender, State & Society*, 30(3), p. 818 (Oxford Academic, 2023)
- **URL:** https://academic.oup.com/sp/article/30/3/818/6833010
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — descriptive; well-documented pattern

**Finding — cultural assimilation of immigrant women:** Across 23 EU countries 2002–2020, immigrant women's labour supply is positively and robustly associated with the female-to-male labour force participation ratio in their region of residence. Evidence points to **cultural assimilation** — immigrant women adopt the gender norms of host-country women — rather than simple institutional exposure. No equivalent assimilation effect found for immigrant men.
- **Source:** *World Development* / ScienceDirect (2024) — ESS data 2002–2020, 23 EU countries
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — observational; assimilation mechanism plausible but identification is limited

### 2.6 Informal / Grey Economy Participation

**Finding:** Irregular migrants are structurally over-represented in informal economy work. The dominant sectors are agriculture, construction, domestic service, and hospitality. Both migrant workers and employers have incentives to operate informally (cost savings, avoidance of credential barriers). Measuring informal economy participation of irregular migrants remains a fundamental methodological challenge — they are excluded from population registers and official labour force surveys.
- **Source:** Salihoğlu & Vargas-Silva — MIrreM Working Paper No. 5 (2024); Dimitriadis (2023) *Work, Employment and Society*
- **URLs:** https://irregularmigration.eu/pub/MIRREM-Saliho%C4%9Flu%20and%20Vargas-Silva-2024-Irregular%20Migrants%20in%20the%20Informal%20Economy-v1.pdf | https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/09500170211072777
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak quantitatively — structural conditions are documented; scale is not reliably measurable

**Structural vulnerability indicators (Eurostat 2024 — Tier 1 data):**
- **43.8%** of non-EU citizens in the EU face risk of poverty or social exclusion (vs. 18.5% of nationals)
- **22.5%** of non-EU citizens hold temporary employment contracts (vs. 10.9% for nationals)
- **35.2%** of non-EU citizens face poverty risk; **15.0%** experience severe material deprivation
- These structural vulnerabilities create the conditions that push migrants toward grey economy participation
- **Source:** Eurostat Migrant Integration Statistics — Employment Conditions / Poverty (2024)
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Migrant_integration_statistics_-_employment_conditions
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the vulnerability indicators (Tier 1 Eurostat data); 🟧 Weak on causal link to informal participation specifically

### 2.7 Occupational Segregation Patterns

**Finding:** **38.5%** of tertiary-educated migrants in the EU are over-educated for their jobs. Among high-skilled workers, overqualification affects **34% of within-EU migrants** vs. **22% of native workers**. In Italy, over **50% of non-EU migrants aged 25–64** are overqualified; in Portugal and the Netherlands, approximately 20%. Female migrants are disproportionately employed in occupations requiring lower education than obtained.
- **Source:** European Parliament Study (2024) — *"New Approaches to Labour Market Integration of Migrants and Refugees"*; IMF WP 2024/211
- **URL:** https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/STUD/2024/754232/IPOL_STU(2024)754232_EN.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — EU-wide Eurostat-derived data; consistent across studies

**Finding — refugee occupational disadvantage:** Refugees are **11.6% less likely to have a job** and **22% more likely to be unemployed** than comparable non-EU migrants. They have lower income, poorer occupational quality, and lower labour market participation. Disparities **persist up to 10–15 years after arrival**.
- **Source:** Fasani et al. (2022), cited in European Parliament (2024) and CEPR (2023)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — peer-reviewed; consistent with other refugee-specific studies

**Finding — migrant employment gap:** In 2021, migrants in Europe were on average **8.6 percentage points less likely to be employed** than nationals with similar individual characteristics. The gap narrows substantially with duration: migrants with under 5 years' residence show a **17.7 pp gap**; those with 6+ years show a **7 pp gap**.
- **Source:** Frattini & Dalmonte (2024), cited in European Parliament Study (2024)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — cited as forthcoming; methodology consistent with established literature

**Finding — labour shortage sectors:** Migrant worker increases in shortage sectors 2019–2022 substantially exceeded native worker increases: building/construction trades (+7% migrants vs. stable for natives); health sector (+15% migrants vs. +7% natives); ICT (+50% migrants vs. +30% natives).
- **Source:** European Labour Authority (ELA), cited in European Parliament (2024)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — administrative data; directional finding is robust

### 2.8 Overall Labour Market Context

**Finding:** Of **4.2 million EU jobs created between 2019 Q4 and 2023 Q4**, approximately **2.7 million (64%)** were filled by non-EU citizens — the highest share in recent EU history. This occurred while EU-citizen unemployment remained at historic lows. Non-EU citizens' share in total EU employment roughly doubled from ~5% to ~10% between 2005 and 2023. Non-EU migrant employment rate in 2024: **65.3%** (vs. 71.4% for natives; 74.9% for intra-EU movers).
- **Source:** European Commission (2024) — *"Migration, Mobility and the EU Labour Market"*; OECD International Migration Outlook 2025
- **URLs:** https://economy-finance.ec.europa.eu/migration-mobility-and-eu-labour-market_en | https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/international-migration-outlook-2025_ae26c893-en.html
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — Tier 1 administrative and LFS data

**OECD earnings gap:** Immigrant earnings on entry are **34% below** comparable native-born workers of the same age and gender. This gap narrows to **21%** within five years. Approximately **two-thirds** of the entry earnings gap is due to immigrants working in lower-paying sectors and firms — not lower within-job wages.
- **Source:** OECD International Migration Outlook 2025
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — cross-national OECD data

## Counter-Evidence & Debates

**Against negative wage effects at the bottom:** Card and Peri argue the Borjas skill-cell methodology overstates wage compression because it misclassifies native high school students as high school dropouts and assumes perfect substitutability. The spatial/area approach finds near-zero effects. The debate turns on untestable assumptions about substitution elasticity.

**Against negligible wage effects:** Borjas and Edo (2021) demonstrate that studies using wage samples exclude native workers (especially women) who exit employment due to immigration-induced competition. This creates systematic upward bias in wage-effect estimates. Correcting for selection produces larger negative effects, particularly for women. This is a methodologically valid critique that has not been fully resolved.

**On displacement:** The IZA literature notes that immigrants do not simply displace native workers because labour markets are segmented and immigrants enter lower tiers. However, Edo & Ozguzel (2023) document real short-run displacement of low-educated natives — the segmentation argument does not make displacement effects zero.

---

## Confounding Factors

- **Labour market institutions (EPL):** This is the dominant moderating variable in EU-specific analyses. Displacement and wage effects are substantially smaller in countries with strong collective bargaining and employment protection. Results from flexible-labour-market countries (UK, Denmark) should not be generalised to rigid ones (Italy, Spain, France).
- **Economic cycle:** Immigration's labour market effects are more adverse during recessions (when complementarity via job creation is limited) and less adverse during expansions. Studies covering the 2008–2012 recession period show larger negative effects than pre- or post-recession analyses.
- **Immigrant skill level:** The direction of wage and employment effects reverses between high-skilled and low-skilled immigration. Aggregate figures that pool skill groups conceal opposing effects.
- **Native mobility:** Borjas argued that native workers respond to immigration by relocating away from high-immigration regions, distributing adverse effects nationally. If this occurs, regional studies understate wage effects. Evidence for strong native mobility in Europe is weaker than in the US.
- **Occupation downgrading:** Immigrants' arrival below their skill level means they compete with different workers than their qualifications would suggest, reducing displacement effects on native workers at their qualification level while concentrating pressure on lower-skilled workers and prior immigrants.

## Evidence Gaps

1. **EU-specific wage effect estimates with correction for native mobility and selection bias.** Most EU wage studies do not apply Borjas & Edo's selection correction. The magnitude of the bias in EU studies is unknown.
2. **Informal economy scale.** The proportion of irregular migrants in informal work is structurally difficult to measure. Estimates from regularisation surveys (Spain, Italy) are the best available but are sporadic.
3. **Long-run refugee labour market outcomes beyond 15 years.** Fasani et al. (2022) show disparities persist up to 10–15 years. Whether full convergence occurs beyond that point is not established.
4. **Post-2022 Ukrainian cohort labour market trajectory.** The cohort is distinct (majority female, higher education, temporary protection status). Employment integration rates vary widely across receiving countries. Long-run outcomes unknown.
5. **Second-generation labour market outcomes by origin group.** The persistent employment gap of second-generation immigrants (documented in CEPR VoxEU 2023) is not well-explained; discrimination, geography, and social networks all contribute but relative importance is unresolved.

## Bottom Line

The average wage effect of immigration on native workers in the EU is **near zero**, but distributional consequences are real and asymmetric: workers below the 20th percentile of the wage distribution face negative pressure; workers above the median benefit from complementarities. Studies that ignore distributional effects misrepresent the impact on the workers most vulnerable to competition.

Employment displacement is **modest and short-lived** at the aggregate level, but meaningful for low-educated native workers in the short run, particularly in rigid-EPL countries. Labour market institutions are the key moderating variable: the EU's wide variation in EPL means there is no single "EU effect" — outcomes diverge significantly between flexible and rigid systems.

Immigration has been **a major contributor to EU labour force growth** in 2019–2023: 64% of net new jobs were filled by non-EU citizens during a period of historic low EU-citizen unemployment. This filling-of-shortages function is not disputed.

The weakest part of the evidence base is the treatment of **gender and selection bias** in wage studies. Borjas & Edo (2021) raise a valid methodological concern that existing EU wage studies may systematically overstate the absence of negative effects on women who exit the labour market.

**Confidence level:** High on the distributional wage finding (UK, replicated). High on employment data (Tier 1). Moderate on the quantitative magnitude of wage effects (methodological disagreement unresolved). Moderate on complementarity benefits (EPL-dependent). Low on informal economy scale.
