# Domain 7: Political Consequences

**Research date:** 2026-02-22
**Evidence covers:** Electoral effects of migration, voter shifts, public opinion, EU institutional responses, Schengen stress, democratic legitimacy gap, political polarisation
**Primary sources:** Review of Economic Studies, AJPS, ifo/EconPol, Eurobarometer, ESS, EC institutional documents, Frontex

---

## Evidence Summary

The political consequences of mass migration in the EU are extensive and empirically documented across multiple dimensions. Immigration volume and rate of demographic change are positively correlated with radical right vote shares at the regional level across most European countries, with causal evidence established for Denmark, Austria, and with null or inverted effects documented in urban German contexts. Public dissatisfaction with EU migration policy is broad and persistent: 51% of Europeans rate EU migration policy negatively (Eurobarometer 2024); 68% overestimate the non-EU migrant share of their population. The EU's core migration governance instrument (the Dublin Regulation) effectively collapsed in 2015 and has not been replaced with a functioning alternative until the New Pact (operative 2026, contested). Schengen's internal border controls have proliferated to near-normalcy: 472 notifications since 2006, with 10–11 member states maintaining controls as of early 2026. Political polarisation on the cultural dimension (identity, immigration, cosmopolitanism vs. nativism) is rising and increasingly displacing the traditional economic left-right axis.

## Key Findings

### 7.1 Rise of Populist/Anti-Immigration Parties — Causal Evidence

**Finding (Denmark — strongest causal study):** In non-urban municipalities, allocation of larger refugee shares between electoral cycles **causally increases** vote share for right-leaning anti-immigration parties and shifts centre-right/centre-left balance rightward. In large urban municipalities, the effect is null or reversed.
- **Source:** Dustmann, Vasiljeva & Damm — *Review of Economic Studies*, Vol. 86(5), 2019, pp. 2035–2070. Quasi-random dispersal policy as natural experiment; fully controlled.
- **URL:** https://academic.oup.com/restud/article-abstract/86/5/2035/5112970
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — quasi-random dispersal; strongest causal identification in the literature; peer-reviewed in top journal

**Finding (Austria — contact vs. transit):** Direct presence of refugees in a municipality is *negatively* correlated with FPÖ support. But municipalities at the German border experiencing mass transit inflows showed *increased* FPÖ vote shares. Type of exposure (personal contact vs. transit flow) is the decisive variable.
- **Source:** Steinmayr (2021) — IV regressions; 2015 Upper Austrian state elections
- **URL:** https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/EconPol_Policy_Report_23_Immigration_Far_Right.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — causal identification via IV; peer-reviewed

**Finding (Germany — rural null effect):** In rural Eastern Germany, despite widespread anti-immigrant sentiment, local refugee presence does **not** significantly increase right-wing vote shares. Sociotropic perceptions (concerns about the country generally), not local exposure, drive attitudes. Elite and media framing mediates local exposure effects.
- **Source:** Causal forest analysis; administrative allocation rules; EconPol Policy Report 2019 (ifo Institute)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — working paper / policy report; consistent with peer-reviewed Austrian findings

**Finding (Berlin — contact theory supported):** Spatial proximity to asylum seekers is *negatively* correlated with AfD vote shares in the 2019 European Parliament elections in Berlin; effect is stronger in higher-income districts.
- **Source:** Hangartner et al. — *Journal of Ethnic and Migration Studies* (2022); spatial regression; geo-localised data
- **URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1369183X.2022.2100543
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — correlational with spatial controls; single-city study

**Finding (skill level of immigrants):** Inflows of low-skilled non-EU immigrants tend to increase support for extreme right parties; high-skilled immigrant inflows have no effect or reduce far-right support.
- **Source:** EconPol Policy Report (ifo Institute, 2019)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — correlation with controls; causal mechanism plausible but contested

**Finding (intra-EU migration):** Central and Eastern European immigration presence is positively associated with Eurosceptic party vote shares in Western EU host countries across 30 elections in 7 countries.
- **Source:** Toshkov — *European Union Politics*, SAGE (2024); panel data; controls for confounders
- **URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/14651165241271979
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — controlled correlation; falls short of causal identification

### 7.2 Voter Shifts: Who Moved and Why

**Finding — class realignment:** Working-class voters across multiple EU countries have shifted from left to right parties, partly driven by a shift in social identity from class-based to culture/identity-based politics. France's Rassemblement National expanded from 1.39% (2017) to over 21% (2024) with a strong working-class component.
- **Source:** Atlas Institute for International Affairs; electoral data
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the descriptive electoral trend; 🟫 Moderate on causal attribution to migration specifically

**Finding — generational shift (2024 EP elections):** Young voters (under 25) who supported the Greens in 2019 shifted substantially rightward by 2024. Germany: 16–17% of under-25 voters backed the AfD in 2024 (up ~11 pp from 2019). France: 32% of voters aged 25–34 backed National Rally in 2024. **This contradicts the Norris-Inglehart cultural backlash thesis**, which predicted generational replacement would erode populist support.
- **Source:** Blavatnik School of Government (Oxford); The Local (January 2026); ESS data
- **URLs:** https://www.bsg.ox.ac.uk/blog/why-young-europeans-are-embracing-far-right
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the electoral data; the causal attribution to migration specifically is 🟫 Moderate

**Finding — gender gap:** The far-right surge among young voters is disproportionately driven by young men: over 21% of young men voted far-right in 2024 EP elections vs. approximately 14% of young women. Spain: 36% of young men backed Vox vs. 25% of under-24 voters overall.
- **Source:** Blavatnik School, Oxford
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the descriptive gap; mechanism unresolved

**Finding — emigration as hidden driver:** Out-migration (emigration) from depopulating peripheral regions leaves behind older, more conservative, more nativist voter pools, increasing radical right vote shares — *independent of immigration inflows*. Tested across 28 European countries.
- **Source:** Dancygier et al. — *American Journal of Political Science* (2025)
- **URL:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajps.12852
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — strong theoretical mechanism; causal arrow suggested but not established via experiment

**Finding — inequality-driven surge:** When the poorest citizens lose a significant share of national income, far-right vote shares rise, using decades of national and individual-level data.
- **Source:** Sudbrack & Downes, ECPR The Loop
- **URL:** https://theloop.ecpr.eu/rising-inequality-is-driving-europes-far-right-surge/
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — established correlation; authors argue causal channel via economic grievance

**Finding — radical right "issue ownership":** Radical right parties hold stable electoral "issue ownership" on immigration. The more immigration rises on the political agenda, the more these parties benefit. Mainstream parties adopt more restrictive immigration positions after radical right electoral success ("contagion effect").
- **Source:** *Frontiers in Political Science* (2025); Van Spanje (2010); Mudde (2013)
- **URL:** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/political-science/articles/10.3389/fpos.2025.1529840/full
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — observational; contagion effect is disputed in magnitude by Mudde

### 7.3 Public Opinion Data

**Eurobarometer 2024 (Tier 1 survey):**
- **51%** of Europeans rate EU migration policy "negatively"; only 16% positive
- **59%** want fighting irregular migration to be an EU priority — 4th on overall agenda, above climate and Ukraine aid
- **68%** overestimate the non-EU migrant share of their country's population (average guess: 16%; actual: under 7%)
- **Source:** Standard Eurobarometer 101, Spring 2024
- **URL:** https://veriangroup.com/hubfs/BE/Eurobarometer/Standard-101-Spring%202024.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — Tier 1 survey; 27,000+ respondents across EU27

**European Social Survey (Wave 11, 2023):**
- Long-run trend: acceptance of migration rose from 2002–2020 (24% to 37% thinking immigrants improve their country), then plateaued/slightly declined to 36% in 2023
- Country-level variation is extreme: most accepting — Iceland, Finland, Switzerland, UK; least accepting — Greece, Hungary, Slovakia
- Ireland: improved +8 pp in most recent wave; Sweden: +16 pp
- **Source:** ESS Wave 11; RFBerlin analysis
- **URL:** https://www.rfberlin.com/attitudes-towards-migration-in-europe/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — longitudinal survey design; 31-country coverage

**Perception gap:** Europeans consistently *overestimate* migrant population size. Public concern over migration is highest in countries with fewer actual migrants — suggesting media salience and political framing matter as much as actual exposure.
- **Source:** Migration Data Portal synthesis; Eurobarometer
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — replicated across surveys; consistent pattern

### 7.4 EU Institutional Responses and Their Effectiveness

**Dublin Regulation — effective failure:**
- The "first country of entry" principle placed disproportionate burden on frontline states (Greece, Italy, Spain, Malta, Cyprus)
- In 2015, frontline states stopped enforcing Dublin, allowing secondary movements to Germany and Sweden — a de facto system collapse
- The European Commission declared in 2020: "There is currently no effective solidarity mechanism in place, and no efficient rule on responsibility"
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — institutional documents and Commission assessments are unambiguous

**EU-Turkey Statement (March 2016):**
- Key terms: irregular arrivals on Greek islands returned to Turkey; 1:1 resettlement for returns; €6 billion EU commitment; Turkish visa liberalisation
- Effectiveness: arrivals in Greece dropped sharply after March 2016. Actual returns executed: ~2,000 (Greek courts rejected Turkey as "safe third country"). Only 25,000 of 72,000 agreed resettlement places were used
- Political effects: Turkey gained geopolitical leverage over EU, regularly threatening to "open borders" for concessions. Normalised externalisation as EU migration strategy. Became template for subsequent agreements (Libya, Tunisia)
- **Source:** Migration Policy Institute; CIDOB; ODI
- **URLs:** https://www.migrationpolicy.org/article/eu-turkey-deal-five-years-on
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on measured outcomes (arrivals, returns, resettlements); 🟫 Moderate on long-term political effects

**New Pact on Migration and Asylum (adopted May 2024; operative June 2026):**
- Replaces Dublin III with Asylum and Migration Management Regulation (AMMR)
- Introduces "mandatory solidarity mechanism": up to 30,000 relocations per year or financial equivalents
- Pre-entry screening, expedited border procedures, and accelerated returns
- **Academic assessment:** Over 200 academics from 66 European universities called it "inhumane." AMMR described as "evil twin" of Dublin — preserving first-country-of-entry logic with added procedural restrictions
- **Advocacy position (Tier 5, context only):** Oxfam, Caritas, Amnesty, Save the Children signed joint condemnation
- **Source:** Delors Centre; EU Parliament; IRC; Oxford Law Blogs
- **URL:** https://www.delorscentre.eu/en/publications/the-eu-migration-pact-at-two
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — too recent for outcome measurement; academic critiques are well-reasoned but pre-implementation

### 7.5 Schengen Area Stress

**Statistics (EC Home Affairs — Tier 1):**
- 2006–July 2025: **472 total notifications** of temporary internal border control reimpositions
- 2006–2014: 1–8 notifications per year (mainly for summits)
- 2015: Sharp surge — 7 Schengen states reimposed controls; also linked to Paris and Brussels terrorist attacks
- 2024: 38 notifications
- As of early 2026: **10–11 Schengen states** maintain internal border controls (Austria, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Slovenia, Sweden)
- **Source:** EC Home Affairs; State of Schengen Report 2024 (EUR-Lex)
- **URLs:** https://home-affairs.ec.europa.eu/policies/schengen/schengen-area/temporary-reintroduction-border-control_en | https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:52024DC0173
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — Tier 1 institutional data

**Key events (2024–2025):**
- September 2024: Germany reimposed controls at all land borders (France, Belgium, Netherlands, Poland, Czech Republic, Austria, Switzerland, Denmark), citing irregular migration and crime
- November 2024: France reimposed controls with 6 Schengen neighbours
- December 2024: Netherlands reimposed land border controls

**Legal tension:** ECJ (April 2022) ruled controls cannot exceed 6 months for the same threat; several states' controls since 2015 are potentially unlawful under this interpretation. Regulation (EU) 2024/1717 expanded legitimate grounds to include "large-scale unauthorised movements" — criticised by the Meijers Committee as dangerously vague.

### 7.6 Democratic Legitimacy Gap

**Finding:** Research across Belgium, Denmark, Germany, and the Netherlands finds **little evidence for negative public opinion responsiveness** in legislative activity — governments do not consistently tighten immigration legislation when public opinion turns more hostile. Hollifield's "liberal state hypothesis": even governments espousing strict rhetoric allow relatively high immigration due to economic need, rights frameworks, and international obligations. Populist parties have explicitly exploited this gap, claiming elites ignore "the will of the people" on immigration.
- **Source:** RECONNECT Project; ResearchGate preference-policy gap study; Taylor & Francis (2025)
- **URLs:** https://reconnect-europe.eu/blog/the-paradoxes-of-democratic-legitimacy-in-eu-migration-policies/ | https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277595099_The_Gap_between_Public_Preferences_and_Policies_on_Immigration
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — well-theorised; empirical evidence covers selected countries; causal mechanism plausible

**Structural legitimacy deficit:** EU migration governance faces dual legitimacy deficits. Input legitimacy (democratic accountability) is weak in cross-border migration decisions — externalisation deals (EU-Turkey, EU-Libya, EU-Tunisia) fall outside normal parliamentary scrutiny. Output legitimacy is disputed from both sides: too restrictive for human rights advocates; too permissive for restrictionist publics.

### 7.7 Political Polarisation

**Finding — axis shift:** Party system polarisation is increasingly organised around a "cultural" axis (immigration, identity, cosmopolitanism vs. nativism) rather than the traditional economic left-right axis. This reordering is documented in party manifestos, voter surveys, and electoral outcomes across EU member states.
- **Source:** *European Journal of Political Research* (2025); Taylor & Francis (2024)
- **URL:** https://ejpr.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1475-6765.12718
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the descriptive reordering; 🟫 Moderate on migration specifically as the cause

**Finding — affective polarisation:** Dislike of out-party supporters is rising in European multi-party systems. Migration and cultural issues appear to be its primary fuel, more so than economic issues.
- **Source:** *European Political Science Review* (Cambridge)
- **URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-political-science-review/article/affective-polarization-in-europe/36BDBF804365FE0B350E610E9E7C714E
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — emerging research area; mechanism identification limited

**Finding — structural class divide (Germany, three decades):** Working-class occupational groups express substantially more negative immigration and EU attitudes than upper classes — a persistent, deepening structural divide, not a recent phenomenon.
- **Source:** Taylor & Francis — Opinion Polarisation (2024); German panel data
- **URL:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14616696.2024.2312948
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — longitudinal panel; 30-year span; Germany-specific

**Finding — reverse thermostatic backlash:** When radical right parties succeed electorally, a portion of the general electorate *thermostatically* shifts its stated preferences toward *greater* pro-immigration attitudes (Public Opinion Quarterly, 2023). This does not eliminate polarisation dynamics but complicates simple linear models.
- **Source:** *Public Opinion Quarterly* (Oxford, 2023)
- **URL:** https://academic.oup.com/poq/article/87/4/1013/7471545
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — peer-reviewed; single-country focus

**Finding — party convergence (not divergence) over 50 years:** Contrary to the polarisation narrative, across European parties over 50 years there is *no consistent left-right polarisation on immigration at the party level* — parties have largely co-moved, often converging toward greater restriction. Elite-level polarisation is thus weaker than voter-level polarisation.
- **Source:** Dancygier & Margalit (2020) — *Comparative Political Studies*; SAGE
- **URL:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0010414019858936
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — 50-year cross-national; well-identified

## Counter-Evidence & Debates

**Against "immigration causes populism":** Dancygier's 50-year analysis shows parties have *converged*, not diverged, on immigration — undermining the simple narrative that immigration produces left-right party polarisation. Economic inequality (Sudbrack & Downes), depopulation and emigration (Dancygier et al. 2025), and institutional distrust are independent drivers of populist voting that are confounded with migration effects in most studies.

**Against Norris-Inglehart:** The *Cultural Backlash* thesis predicted that generational replacement (younger cohorts being more liberal) would erode populist support. The 2024 EP election data showing young men shifting strongly toward the far right constitutes a significant empirical challenge to this prediction. Critics argue the theory needs revision or that the mechanism operates but is overwhelmed by other forces (economic anxiety, social media, gender dynamics).

**Against dismissing the legitimacy gap:** Hollifield and Joppke argue that democracies *structurally* cannot match public restrictionist preferences due to constitutional rights commitments and economic interests. This is a real constraint — not a conspiracy or incompetence — but it does produce a documented gap between stated public preferences and policy outcomes that populists exploit effectively.

**Against "contact reduces prejudice":** The Austrian and Berlin findings (contact reduces far-right support) conflict with the Danish finding (refugee presence increases it) and with the fact that anti-immigration attitudes are highest in areas with fewest actual immigrants. Resolution: type of contact (structured vs. transit exposure), geographic context (urban vs. rural), and local economic conditions all moderate the contact effect. No single finding generalises.

## Confounding Factors

- **Economic conditions:** Far-right vote shares correlate with economic anxiety, unemployment, and inequality independently of immigration. Distinguishing migration-specific from economic grievance effects is methodologically difficult.
- **Media framing:** Public perceptions of migration are shaped at least as much by media coverage as by personal experience. Studies show crime fears uncorrelated with actual victimisation. Controlling for media exposure is rarely done.
- **Pre-existing attitudes:** Areas with pre-existing authoritarian and nativist attitudes show stronger migration-voting effects. Causality may run from pre-existing attitudes to the *perception* of migration as threatening, not from migration itself.
- **Party system structure:** In countries with proportional representation, anti-immigration parties can gain legislative representation at lower vote thresholds, making migration→vote share→policy effects more visible. Westminster systems (UK) show the same attitudinal shifts but different electoral expression.
- **Type of migration:** Studies that find effects typically involve asylum seekers or visible refugee populations; studies of skilled labour migration rarely find similar effects. Effect heterogeneity is large.

## Evidence Gaps

1. **Causal mechanisms for the legitimacy gap.** The gap between public preferences and policy outcomes is documented but the causal path from gap → populist vote → policy change is not well-identified. Does populist electoral success actually change immigration policy, or do populist parties converge toward establishment positions once in government?
2. **Long-run electoral effects of integration success.** Studies focus on short-term electoral effects of refugee arrival. Whether successful integration (visible second-generation success) reduces far-right support is not tested.
3. **Media and social media mediation.** No rigorous EU-wide study has identified the causal contribution of media framing to the migration-politics nexus, separate from actual migration volumes.
4. **New Pact outcomes.** The New Pact became operative June 2026. Its effects on actual migration flows, border control, and political attitudes are not yet measurable.
5. **Gender-specific political radicalisation drivers.** The young male far-right shift is documented but poorly explained. Whether this is driven by anti-feminist backlash, economic precarity, or migration-specific concerns is unresolved.

## Bottom Line

Migration is a *major* driver of contemporary EU political dynamics, but it is neither the sole cause of populist politics nor mechanically linked to any single electoral outcome. The causal evidence is clearest for the Danish case: concentrated refugee settlement in non-urban areas causally increases right-wing vote shares. Across other contexts, type of contact, economic conditions, media environment, and urban/rural geography all moderate the effect substantially.

Public dissatisfaction with EU migration policy is real, broad, and persistent — and is compounded by a documented legitimacy gap between what citizens say they want and what EU institutions deliver. This gap is not primarily a failure of democracy but a structural feature of liberal states bound by rights commitments and economic interests that constrain policy options. Populist parties have turned this structural constraint into their most powerful political asset.

Schengen is under structural stress: the internal border control architecture is being normalised in ways that are legally contested and economically costly. The New Pact represents the EU's most serious attempt to create a functioning solidarity mechanism, but it is too early to assess its effectiveness and it faces substantial legal and political challenges.

The Norris-Inglehart cultural backlash thesis — generational replacement would reduce populism — has been empirically challenged by 2024 data showing young voters, especially young men, shifting toward the far right across EU countries.

**Confidence level:** High on public opinion data (Tier 1 surveys). High on Schengen statistics (Tier 1 institutional data). High on Danish causal finding (quasi-random dispersal). Moderate on other electoral causal claims (context-dependent; identification quality varies). Moderate on polarisation mechanisms (well-documented patterns; causal attribution to migration specifically is contested). Low on long-run political equilibrium effects.
