# Domain 4: Public Services

**Research date:** 2026-02-22
**Evidence covers:** Healthcare, Education, Housing, Social Security, Municipal fiscal effects
**Primary sources used:** OECD, Eurostat, IZA, CESifo, PMC/academic journals, national statistical agencies

---

## Evidence Summary

The best available evidence shows that migration's impact on public services is **strongly differentiated by migration motive**. Labour migrants — particularly intra-EU and skilled extra-EU — impose low service costs and make net positive fiscal contributions. Asylum seekers and family-reunification migrants impose significantly higher short- and medium-term costs, principally because their labour market integration is slower and less complete. Housing pressure is real and documented across Germany, the Netherlands, and Sweden, but is multi-causal; immigration is a demand-side contributor alongside dominant supply-side failures. Healthcare systems in most EU countries are *net beneficiaries* of migration through workforce supply, while education systems face documented achievement gaps and language-support costs.

## Key Findings

### 4.1 Healthcare

**Finding:** Migrants in the UK use *fewer* NHS services than native-born populations, primarily because they are younger and have fewer pre-existing conditions. This pattern is consistent across most EU countries for newly arrived migrants.
- **Source:** BMC Public Health / PMC, cross-sectional analysis of two UK national health surveys (2020)
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7734958/
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — UK data; EU-wide equivalent studies are limited due to data gaps (see 4.1b)

**Finding (4.1b):** EU-wide health information systems systematically fail to capture migrant-disaggregated health data, making robust EU-level measurement of healthcare demand attributable to migrants impossible.
- **Source:** Lancet Regional Health — Europe, systematic review (Bielefeld University / Heidelberg, 2023)
- **URL:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10625017/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on the data-gap finding itself

**Finding (4.1c):** The NHS is structurally dependent on migrant labour: 21% of total NHS workforce, 38% of GMC-registered doctors, and 29% of nurses are foreign-born (2023). NHS Confederation concludes migrants are a net benefit to the NHS as workforce contributors.
- **Source:** NHS Confederation; Migration Observatory (Oxford); CQC State of Care 2022–23
- **URLs:** https://www.nhsconfed.org/articles/immigration-harming-nhs | https://migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk/resources/briefings/migration-and-the-health-and-care-workforce/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — administrative workforce data

**Finding (4.1d):** Ukrainian refugee wave (2022–present) — 5+ million arrivals in EU in months — acutely strained healthcare systems in front-line receiving countries (Poland, Germany, Czech Republic). No robust quantification of system-level impact yet available.
- **Source:** MDPI Sustainability (2022)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟧 Weak on quantification; event itself is documented

### 4.2 Education

**Finding:** Immigrant pupils in Germany score approximately **59 points lower in Mathematics** and **67 points lower in Reading** than native German children on PISA assessments — a gap equivalent to roughly two grade levels.
- **Source:** IZA World of Labor, *"Migrants and Educational Achievement Gaps"* (Entorf, Goethe University); OECD PISA data (2022–23)
- **URL:** https://wol.iza.org/uploads/articles/146/pdfs/migrants-and-educational-achievement-gaps.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — replicated across PISA waves; consistent cross-nationally

**Finding:** Immigrant-student concentration has a **small negative to zero effect** on native children's academic outcomes. Effects are primarily linked to the socioeconomic disadvantage of incoming students, not migration per se. Language-support and resource provision can largely offset any negative effects.
- **Source:** IZA World of Labor, *"Immigrants in the Classroom and Effects on Native Children"*; OECD PISA cross-country study
- **URL:** https://wol.iza.org/uploads/articles/194/pdfs/immigrants-in-classroom-and-effects-on-native-children.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — cross-country comparisons have methodological limitations; findings not uniform

**Finding (Eurostat 2024):** Non-EU citizens have low educational attainment at more than **twice the rate** of EU nationals. Adults aged 25–34 born outside the EU achieve tertiary degrees at 38.4% vs. 45.2% for country-born peers. The gap has narrowed 3.7 percentage points since 2015.
- **Source:** Eurostat, *Migrant Integration Statistics — Education* (2024)
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Migrant_integration_statistics_-_education
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — Tier 1 statistical data

**Finding (Sweden SFI):** Sweden's mandatory Swedish-for-Immigrants programme enrolled 163,000+ students in 2017 (8.7% year-on-year increase). Some refugee cohorts arrive entirely illiterate, requiring reading instruction from scratch. Fully municipality-funded. The 2016 state budget added €200 million for early integration covering language, interpreters, and vocational training.
- **Source:** Skolverket national statistics; Nordic Council 2025 review
- **URL:** https://pub.norden.org/nord2025-038/sweden.html
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on enrolment data; cost-effectiveness of programme is 🟫 Moderate

**Finding (Belgium):** Belgium has the most heavily segregated school system in the EU. Schools with predominantly immigrant-background pupils show poorer educational standards. School overcrowding and teacher shortages are operational concerns.
- **Source:** Eurochild Report, *"Challenges to Inclusive and Accessible Education in Europe"* (2024)
- **URL:** https://eurochild.org/uploads/2024/02/Sub-report-Education-2024.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — policy report; limited primary data quantification

### 4.3 Housing

**Finding:** EU house prices rose **53% on average** from 2015 to 2024. Peak growth was 8% annually in 2021–22. In 2024, EU households spent on average 19% of disposable income on housing.
- **Source:** Eurostat Housing Interactive Publication 2025
- **URL:** https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/interactive-publications/housing-2025
- **Note:** Migration is one demand-side contributor; dominant drivers are supply-side (construction costs, interest rates, regulation)
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on price data; attribution to migration specifically is 🟫 Moderate

**Finding:** Immigration inflows have a statistically significant adverse effect on **rental prices** across 382 German districts. Effect is attenuated when refugees are dispersed to decentralised rather than centralised accommodation.
- **Source:** Panel regression study across 382 German districts with instrumental variable approach (2023)
- **URL:** https://www.researchgate.net/publication/367641812_The_Effect_of_Immigration_on_Housing_Prices_Evidence_from_382_German_Districts
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — single-country study; methodology is sound

**Finding (Netherlands):** Netherlands has a shortage of approximately **401,000 homes** (2024). Average social housing waiting time is 7 years. Rents rose 5.4% year-on-year in July 2024 — the largest increase since 1993. Net migration is identified as a primary demand driver alongside smaller household formation.
- **Source:** ABN AMRO Housing Market Monitor; Euronews (December 2024)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — causal attribution to migration specifically vs. broader demand factors requires further isolation

**Finding (Sweden):** Housing completions fell **54.5%** between 2021 and 2024 — the largest decline in the EUROCONSTRUCT network. Housing costs consumed 25% of Swedish household disposable income.
- **Source:** EUROCONSTRUCT (2024)
- **URL:** https://www.euroconstruct.org/news/housing-under-pressure-how-politics-policy-and-investment-are-shaping-europes-housing-future/
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on supply-side collapse; migration's demand contribution is 🟫 Moderate

**Finding (Segregation):** A comparative study of Belgium, Denmark, Netherlands, and Sweden using geo-coded individual register data found **moderate but consistent** residential over-representation of non-European migrants. Belgium shows the most pronounced patterns; variation across countries is primarily driven by housing policy and refugee dispersal rules, not self-selection alone.
- **Source:** *European Journal of Population* (Springer, 2019)
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10680-018-9481-5
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — multi-country, rigorous methodology; data is from 2019

### 4.4 Social Security / Welfare

**Finding (Germany):** 45% of nationals from asylum-originating countries receive *Bürgergeld* (welfare). Unemployment rate for this group: **30.5%** vs. 2.3% for native Germans (June 2024). Overall, 11% of all foreigners in Germany receive welfare; only 1.7% of native Germans do. Federal refugee-related spending: **€28.4 billion in 2024** (+40% since 2014); state spending: an additional **€6.7 billion** (+179% since 2014).
- **Source:** MIWI Institute analysis of German Federal Employment Agency data; German federal budget (2024)
- **URL:** https://miwi-institut.de/archives/3316
- **Orientation note:** MIWI Institute — market-oriented, critical of welfare expansion. Cross-check: German federal budget figures are independently confirmable.
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong on headline welfare/unemployment figures (administrative data); 🟫 Moderate on total cost estimates

**Finding (Sweden):** Asylum seekers cost the Swedish state a net **€4.4 billion in 2015 alone** (~1% of GDP). Per-refugee annual fiscal cost: €5,600–€9,900. Lifetime cumulative cost per refugee: **€324,800–€574,200** (58-year post-arrival horizon).
- **Source:** University of Gothenburg study commissioned by Swedish Ministry of Finance (2019)
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — strong methodology but 2019 study; integration rates have shifted since

**Finding (Denmark):** Danish Ministry of Finance (2021): immigrants from MENA, Pakistan, and Turkey (MENAPT) cost on average **−€14,200 per person per year** in net fiscal terms. Western immigrants contribute **+€4,700 per person per year**. Total annual net cost of MENAPT immigration: **€3.6 billion**.

| Group | Annual net fiscal contribution |
|---|---|
| Danish citizens | +€1,100 |
| Western immigrants | +€4,700 |
| MENAPT-origin immigrants | −€14,200 |

- **Source:** Danish Ministry of Finance (2021); confirmed in *Journal of Population Economics* (Springer)
- **URL:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00148-017-0636-1
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — government administrative data; replicated in peer-reviewed literature

**Finding (Netherlands — IZA 2025):** Only **20% of all immigrants** to the Netherlands make a positive lifetime net fiscal contribution. By motive:
- Labour migrants: +€100,000 to +€210,000 lifetime
- Family reunification migrants: approximately −€200,000 lifetime
- Asylum seekers: approximately −€400,000 lifetime (highest for African and Middle Eastern origin)

The study uses individual-level fiscal contribution and benefit receipt data for the *entire* Dutch population (not a sample) and discounted lifetime net contribution calculations across 87 source regions.
- **Source:** IZA Discussion Paper No. 17569, Van de Beek, Hartog, Kreffer, Roodenburg (University of Amsterdam, 2025)
- **URLs:** https://docs.iza.org/dp17569.pdf | https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5077481
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — individual-level population data; rigorous microsimulation; peer-reviewed

**Finding (EU-15 study):** Across 15 EU countries 2007–2018, intra-EU migrants maintain a statistically significantly *positive* net fiscal position relative to natives. Extra-EU migrants show a positive gross position but a **negative net position** once age, education, and family structure are controlled for. Both groups' welfare benefit receipt declined relative to natives over the period.
- **Source:** *International Tax and Public Finance* (Springer, 2024–2025); Transeuroworks Working Paper
- **URLs:** https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10797-025-09942-4 | https://transeuroworks.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/The-net-fiscal-position-of-migrants-in-Europe_WP.pdf
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — EU-SILC data has known limitations; but multi-country replication increases confidence

**Finding (OECD 2024):** Over 2014–2018, migrants in European countries contributed approximately **€1,500 more per capita annually** than natives on average. This average conceals large variation: labour migration is net positive; study, family, and asylum streams are net negative. OECD's long-term overall fiscal impact range: **−1% to +1% of GDP** (average near neutral).
- **Source:** OECD International Migration Outlook 2024
- **URL:** https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/international-migration-outlook-2024_50b0353e-en.html
- **Evidence strength:** ⬛ Strong — OECD synthesis across 38 member states

### 4.5 Municipal / Local Government

**Finding (Germany — district level):** An increase in the foreign population share at German district level does **not significantly affect** public investment spending or aggregate tax revenues on average, per a 2024 machine-learning panel study of all German districts 2010–2019. The authors caution this masks finer-grained effects at the municipal level; Germany requires at least **16 years of immigrant residence** before fiscal contributions exceed those of comparable native residents.
- **Source:** CESifo Working Paper No. 11162, Maxand & Sallam (ifo Institute, June 2024)
- **URL:** https://www.cesifo.org/en/publications/2024/working-paper/local-fiscal-effects-immigration-germany
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — rigorous methodology; district-level data may mask municipal heterogeneity

**Finding (Sweden — rural municipalities):** In demographically declining rural Swedish municipalities, refugee settlement has *reversed* fiscal deterioration — re-opening closed schools, filling job vacancies, and restoring local tax revenues. OECD (2018) confirms this pattern.
- **Source:** OECD *"Working Together for Local Integration of Migrants and Refugees"* (2018); confirmed in 2023 policy reviews
- **URL:** https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/working-together-for-local-integration-of-migrants-and-refugees_9789264085350-en.html
- **Evidence strength:** 🟫 Moderate — case study evidence; not generalisable to urban settings

## Counter-Evidence & Debates

**On welfare usage:** The standard critique of gross welfare-use statistics is that they do not control for age structure and recency of arrival. Young, recently arrived migrants who have not yet completed language training or credential recognition are expected to use benefits temporarily. Studies that control for these factors (e.g., the EU-15 Transeuroworks study) show a narrower gap. However, the Netherlands IZA 2025 study — which does control comprehensively and uses individual-level population data — still finds 80% of immigrants have a negative lifetime net contribution, with especially large deficits for asylum and family streams. This is among the most methodologically rigorous studies available and is difficult to dismiss.

**On education effects on native children:** Some studies find zero or negligible effects of immigrant concentration on native children's outcomes; others find small negative effects concentrated in low-income areas. There is no evidence of large negative effects on native children. The stronger finding is the persistent achievement gap *within* migrant pupil populations, which represents both an integration challenge and a long-term labour market cost.

**On housing:** Some economists argue that immigration *also generates labour supply* in construction, potentially increasing housing supply and partially offsetting its demand effects. This effect has not been robustly quantified for EU housing markets.

---

## Confounding Factors

- **Age:** Migrant populations skew young; younger people use fewer healthcare services and more education services. Age-controlled comparisons are essential.
- **Migration motive:** This is the dominant variable. Labour migrants perform systematically better on all fiscal metrics than asylum seekers or family-reunion migrants. Aggregate "migrant" statistics that do not disaggregate by motive are nearly useless for policy purposes.
- **Time horizon:** Fiscal balance is almost always negative in the first 5 years and trends toward neutral or positive after 15–20 years for most groups, depending on labour market integration success.
- **Pre-existing housing supply failure:** EU housing markets were supply-constrained before the migration surge of 2015–2022. Attribution of current housing costs to migration alone is inappropriate without accounting for construction-sector failures.
- **Welfare system generosity:** Effects are systematically larger in Scandinavian-style welfare states (Denmark, Sweden, Netherlands) than in Southern European states with less generous working-age transfers. The institutional context shapes the fiscal impact.
- **Geographic concentration:** Strain is most acute in specific urban areas (Berlin, Amsterdam, Stockholm, Paris suburbs) rather than uniformly national. National averages obscure local stress points.

## Disaggregated Analysis

| Dimension | Key finding |
|---|---|
| **Intra-EU labour migrants** | Net positive fiscal position; contribute more than native-born per OECD and EU-15 studies |
| **Extra-EU labour migrants (skilled)** | Net positive (Netherlands: +€100,000–210,000 lifetime); contribute significantly to NHS and healthcare workforce |
| **Family-reunification migrants** | Net negative across all country studies (Netherlands: −€200,000 lifetime) |
| **Asylum seekers / refugees** | Net negative short and medium term (Sweden: −€325k–574k lifetime; Netherlands: −€400k lifetime); some long-term convergence if well-integrated |
| **Ukrainian refugees (2022+)** | Special case: high education levels, labour market integration rates >50% in Poland, Lithuania; <25% in Germany, Austria (OECD 2024) |
| **MENAPT-origin immigrants (DK)** | Highest per-person net fiscal cost; −€14,200/year vs. +€4,700 for Western immigrants |
| **Education outcomes** | Persistent PISA gap (~2 grade levels in Germany); gap narrowing in Eurostat attainment data (3.7pp since 2015) |
| **Housing effects** | Documented in Germany (rental prices), Netherlands (social housing queues), Sweden (completion collapse); rural vs. urban outcomes differ sharply |

## Evidence Gaps

1. **EU-wide healthcare demand data** — The Lancet (2023) documents this gap explicitly. Migrant-disaggregated health utilisation data is absent or incomplete in most EU member states.
2. **Long-term education outcomes** — PISA measures attainment at age 15. Long-term outcomes (degree completion, earnings) for second-generation immigrants vary significantly by country and origin, and long-run EU-wide data is limited.
3. **Undocumented migration fiscal impact** — By definition, undocumented migrants are undercounted in official data. Their welfare use is likely low (no eligibility), but their healthcare and education use (particularly for minors) is not well measured.
4. **Housing supply response to migration** — No robust EU study has isolated migration's *net* housing price effect after controlling for migration-driven construction labour supply.
5. **Post-2022 Ukrainian cohort** — Too recent for robust long-term fiscal modelling. Current integration rates vary dramatically by receiving country. Full fiscal picture will not be available for 5–10 years.

---

## Bottom Line

**Migration motive is the decisive variable for public services impact. The evidence for this is strong.**

- Labour migrants, especially intra-EU and skilled extra-EU: net neutral to positive across healthcare workforce, education (their children have gaps but don't measurably harm native peers), housing (no concentrated impact), and fiscal balance.
- Asylum seekers and family-reunification migrants: net negative in the short to medium term across welfare, housing (concentration), education (language costs, gaps), and municipal services. Long-term outcomes depend heavily on labour market integration success, which varies substantially by origin and host-country policy.
- Housing pressure from migration is real and documented but not the primary driver — EU housing markets are structurally supply-constrained, and migration operates as an amplifier of pre-existing dysfunction rather than its cause.
- Healthcare systems in Western EU countries are net beneficiaries of migration as workforce supply, not primarily strained by migrant demand (the exception being acute refugee arrival events like 2015 and 2022).

**Confidence level:** High on the motive-disaggregated fiscal findings (multiple independent studies, individual-level data, replicated across countries). Moderate on housing attribution. Low on healthcare utilisation at EU level due to systematic data absence.
